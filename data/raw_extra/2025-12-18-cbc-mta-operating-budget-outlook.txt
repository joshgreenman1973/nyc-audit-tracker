Executive Summary

The Metropolitan Transportation Authority’s (MTA) November 2025 Financial Plan (Financial Plan), adopted by the Board in December, shows near-term stability—but a close review reveals long-term structural shortfalls and fiscal risks. The plan is balanced in 2025 and 2026 and reports modest gaps of $160 million in 2027, $243 million in 2028, and $306 million in 2029.

However, CBC’s analysis reveals that the MTA’s underlying structural budget gap—the difference between recurring revenues and recurring expenses—is substantially larger, reaching $1.1 billion in 2029. Ensuring reliable service will require the MTA to continue its diligent fiscal and operational management, as well as strong ongoing support from government partners and the public.

The MTA’s financial position has improved significantly since the November 2022 Financial Plan projected a $1.6 billion deficit for 2026. The near-term outlook is supported by stronger-than-expected ridership and tax revenues, and newly-planned operating efficiencies anticipated to save $250 million per year by 2029. 

However, the multi-year plan relies on $2.1 billion in one-time resources, including Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) reimbursements and downstate casino license fees, which will be followed by likely lower recurring gaming tax revenues. The MTA also continues to benefit from prior years’ resources that, once depleted, will no longer be available.

Significant risks also could widen gaps and strain the operating budget. Collectively-bargained annual raises could exceed the 2 percent assumed in the plan. Ridership below projections or persistent fare evasion could dampen revenues. Rapid growth in paratransit trips and costs, uncertain FEMA revenues, and broader economic or federal funding risks also threaten long-term stability. Lastly, if federal capital aid falls below assumptions or the timing of capital borrowing changes, higher debt service costs would create an additional strain.

To ensure the fiscal stability needed to sustain quality service, CBC recommends that the MTA take actions to:

Further increase efficiency, including productivity improvements negotiated with labor and supported by public officials;
Further reduce fare evasion, which importantly relies on the support of government partners and the public;
Contain paratransit cost growth; and 
Continue regular biennial fare and toll increases and consider tying them to growth in operating expenses. 

Redoubling efforts on these fronts now is critical to avoid service reductions, unaffordable debt burdens, or another operating budget bailout in the years ahead.

Introduction

Yesterday, the Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA) Board adopted the November 2025 Financial Plan, establishing the 2026 budget and budgetary forecasts through 2029. 

With higher ridership, increased efficiency, and greater taxpayer support, the MTA’s financial position has improved significantly since the November 2022 Financial Plan projected a $1.6 billion deficit for 2026.1 The November 2025 financial plan is balanced in 2025 and 2026 and projects deficits of $160 million in 2027, $243 million in 2028, and $306 million in 2029.2 Ridership, though still below pre-pandemic levels, is meeting or exceeding expectations. Fare, subsidy, and tax revenues are also meeting or exceeding forecasts. The MTA has already identified $500 million in recurring efficiency savings. 

The State’s 2023 stabilization plan, which provided both one-time and recurring operating budget revenues, improved the MTA’s financial condition but did not solve its budget problems for the long-term.3 CBC finds that the MTA’s $1.1 billion structural budget gap—the difference between recurring revenues and expenses—is more than three times as wide as the reported budget gap. In addition, the Authority faces many risks that could further destabilize its finances, including higher than budgeted collective bargaining agreements, lower than forecast ridership, sustained or elevated fare evasion, economic uncertainty, and rising debt service levels.

Ensuring recurring revenues can sustain recurring expenses is critical not only to the MTA’s fiscal health, but to quality services and the transit-dependent regional economy. To do this, the MTA should continue to reduce fare evasion and secure further efficiency savings in partnership with labor. Smart actions now can avert the future need for harmful service reductions or new revenues. 

Good, at First Glance

At first glance, the November 2025 Financial Plan provides a positive assessment of the MTA’s financial situation: Ridership is recovering faster than expected, tax and subsidy revenues are meeting forecasts, and the reported out-year budget gaps are manageable. (See Table 1.) 

CBC’s review of the MTA’s November 2025 Financial Plan finds that:

The MTA plans to identify recurring operating efficiencies of $75 million in 2026, growing to $250 million by 2029, building on the $500 million in recurring operating efficiencies that the MTA has already implemented;
Labor expenses, 62.0 percent of spending, are in line with the 2025 Adopted Budget; 
Non-labor expenses are $402 million (8.6 percent) above the 2025 Adopted Budget because of higher costs for paratransit contracts, professional service contracts, electricity, and materials;
The MTA has incorporated these higher costs into its baseline budget for 2026 to 2029; 
Paid ridership is meeting or exceeding expectations, with commuter rail at or above 85 percent of pre-pandemic levels and subways at or above 75 percent (Figure 1);
Bridge and tunnel vehicle traffic exceeds both pre-pandemic levels and the MTA’s forecast (Figure 1); 
Fare and toll revenues are tracking with MTA forecasts (Figure 2);
Payroll Mobility Tax (PMT) revenues flowing to the operating budget are greater than expected, driving a $215 million upward revision in 2025 to reflect stronger year-to-date collections; 
The MTA also increased future years’ revenue forecasts;  
The PMT rate increase for the highest payroll businesses, enacted to fund the 2025-2029 Capital Plan, will yield $1.4 billion in 20264 ; and
Forecasts for other taxes and subsidies are largely unchanged. 
Structural Imbalance, At Closer Inspection 

The MTA budget has a $1.1 billion structural imbalance in 2029, despite the MTA’s November 2025 Financial Plan reporting near-term balance and manageable out-year gaps. (See Table 2.) The structural budget gap, the difference between recurring revenues and recurring expenses, is much larger than the reported gaps because the plan uses one-time revenues, prepayments, prior year resources, and optimistic assumptions about future legislative changes. 

In 2026, 2027, and 2028, the plan uses $700 million of one-time reimbursements and fee revenues, which are not available in 2029 or beyond. In 2029, the plan relies on prior year surpluses to cover $682 million in expenses. Lastly, the MTA expects $159 million in higher reimbursement, dependent on legislative action expected in the future. 

One-Time Revenues 
 

The MTA is relying on $2.1 billion in one-time revenues over the next four years. While they reduce short-term financial pressures, they will not be available in future years, thereby masking the gap between recurring revenues and expenses. Key one-time revenues are listed below: 

Federal Reimbursement for COVID Expenses 

The MTA expects to receive $600 million from the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) for COVID-related expenses incurred during the pandemic, budgeted at $200 million a year in 2026, 2027, and 2028. 

Casino Licensing Fees

The State committed downstate casino license fees to the MTA as part of the 2023 stabilization plan. The MTA plans to spend $500 million from each of the three licenses from 2026 through 2028. 

Once operational, the new casinos will provide the MTA with recurring gaming tax revenue. The MTA assumes $100 million in revenue per casino two years after the license fee revenue is realized, amounting to $100 million in 2028, $200 million in 2029, and $300 million in 2030 and thereafter. 

Moving Money Across Years

The MTA sets aside surplus resources to cover future deficits, using them to prepay future expenses or hold them until needed in trust or reserve accounts.  The influx of COVID-related aid allowed the MTA to set aside other resources it continues to use. However, these are non-recurring sources that will not be available after the balance is depleted. 

Debt Service Prepayments

The MTA’s debt service prepayments are projected to reduce debt service expenses by $628 million in 2025 and $285 million in 2026. These are the final prepayments in a series the MTA made coming out of the COVID-19 pandemic. 

Other Postemployment Benefits (OPEB) Prepayments 

The MTA will deposit $37 million into the OPEB Trust fund in 2026 before using $100 million and $300 million from the fund to reduce gaps in 2028 and 2029, respectively. These actions will leave $1.1 billion in the OPEB Trust at the end of 2029. 

While the trust fund is used as a holding account to shift money between years, that is not its purpose. Like a pension fund, it should be used to accumulate the resources needed to fund the MTA’s $21.0 billion OPEB liability.5 

Local Subsidy Drawdown

The MTA shifts unused subsidy dollars across years to decrease out-year budget gaps. It will draw down $125 million this year, $100 million in 2026, and $382 million in 2029. The MTA will also set aside $200 million per year in 2027 and 2028, exhausting the local subsidy resources that have been set aside. Funds will not be available in future years. 

Paratransit Reimbursement Changes 

The Financial Plan assumes the State will enact legislation that will increase New York City’s reimbursement for paratransit. Through June 30, 2027, the City is required to reimburse the MTA 80 percent of paratransit’s net operating expenses or 50 percent plus $165 million—whichever is less.6 The MTA’s Financial Plan assumes the City will instead reimburse the MTA for 80 percent of paratransit’s net operating expenses without a cap.7

The MTA estimates the assumed legislative change would increase the reimbursement by $150 million in 2028.8 Given projected growth in paratransit operating expenses, the increase would be $159 million in 2029.9 

Debt Service Expenses May Strain Operating Budget

Growing debt service costs may strain the operating budget and divert resources from services. The November 2025 Financial Plan projects that operating budget-backed debt service will approach the MTA’s benchmark level of 15 percent of operating expenses in 2029, when debt service expenses will be $3.5 billion (14.7 percent) of the MTA’s $24.0 billion operating budget. While debt service will be below the benchmark, the annual and structural gaps demonstrate the budget’s affordability challenge for all expenses, including debt service.

If labor and non-labor expenses grow at the same rate as the MTA projects over the next four years and the MTA issues new debt as expected, CBC estimates that debt service expenses will exceed 15 percent of operating expenses between 2030 and 2032. (See Figure 3.) 

The 2025-2029 Capital Plan relies on $13 billion of MTA debt.10 Debt service, including this funding, remains below the benchmark as long as the MTA does not need to increase or accelerate its operating-budget-backed borrowing. 

Operating Budget Risks 

Budgets are plans and forecasts; actual results can be higher or lower than expected. To ensure continuation of reliable service and prudently manage the budget, it is imperative the MTA continue to recognize and mitigate significant risks that could further strain finances.  

Labor Negotiations 

The Financial Plan assumes 2 percent annual wage increases for the next round of collective bargaining. Actual wage increases have exceeded 2 percent per year since 2017.11 If either the current LIRR or upcoming Transport Workers Union 100 (TWU) negotiations establish a higher-than-budgeted pattern for the next round, the MTA will incur additional unbudgeted costs.12 Each percentage point above 2 percent would cost $150 million in the first year and compound annually.13  

MTA employees have a strong interest in the MTA’s fiscal stability, since it improves the likelihood of stable employment and competitive wages and benefits.

Most of the $500 million in recurring operating efficiencies implemented since 2023 have not required changes to collective bargaining agreements. But labor is a critical partner for continued progress; many productivity increases require negotiated contract changes. Labor and management should negotiate contracts that increase operational flexibility and employee availability, and share the financial benefits with the employees. The likelihood of this partly depends on greater support from public officials for these changes and MTA efficiency.  

Lower-than-Projected Revenues

In addition to the standard risk inherent in the tax and user fee revenue forecasts, the planned FEMA reimbursement may not materialize. The MTA has sought to collect these FEMA funds since 2020 with no success.14 Although the Authority has reduced how much it expects to receive, these funds may prove uncollectible.  

Ridership and Fare Evasion

The ridership recovery has bolstered the MTA’s budget stability. Any stall or reversal would likely reduce revenue below forecast. The MTA projects that ridership levels 5 percent below forecast would reduce revenues by $325 million.15

The MTA has made important progress and started to turn the tide on fare evasion. Still, evasion remains high, sapping an estimated $900 million in 2025 by CBC’s estimate.16 The MTA should continue its strong and focused efforts. To fully realize the changes needed, these efforts need support by its government partners including the State, New York Police Department, and District Attorneys, and the public. Absent significant progress, fare evasion will present ongoing risk to the operating budget.

Continued Growth in Paratransit Costs  

Paratransit costs have grown rapidly in recent years, spurred by steady, year-over-year ridership growth that has averaged 14.4 percent per year since 2017, excluding 2020 and 2021 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The cost per trip declined slightly over the same period, from $77.45 in 2017 to a projected $76.64 in 2025.17

The Financial Plan assumes slower growth in the number of paratransit trips and a decrease in per-trip costs. If the number of trips grows faster than forecast or if the MTA cannot continue to contain the per-trip cost, paratransit expenses would exceed forecasts and further strain the operating budget.  

Economic Downturn

Taxes and subsidies will support 52.9 percent of the MTA’s operating budget in 2026. The diversity of taxes that support the MTA may buffer it from isolated economic shocks, but a broader economic slowdown or recession could reduce revenues. For example, revenues may fall short if the real estate market slows down, job growth stalls, or consumer spending retracts. The extent and timing of these effects depend on the type of economic shock or recession.  

Federal Risks

The MTA does not depend on the federal government for operating budget resources, aside from the anticipated FEMA reimbursement. However, the federal government has historically provided significant capital funding, which is at risk. The MTA expects $14.0 billion from the federal government to support the 2025-2029 Capital Plan.18 If federal aid falls short, the MTA may delay capital projects, potentially jeopardizing state of good repair work, or issue additional debt to finance those capital projects.  

The federal government is still attempting to rescind approval for New York’s congestion pricing program. The MTA’s lawsuit against the federal government to keep it in place remains unresolved, though a preliminary injunction has allowed the MTA to keep the program running while the case is being decided.19 If the MTA’s lawsuit is unsuccessful and the program is terminated, the MTA will lose most of the revenue it expected to finance $15.0 billion of 2020-2024 Capital Plan.20 

Recommendations

To better align the MTA’s recurring needs and resources, provide reliable service, ensure the long-term stability of the MTA’s operating budget, and prepare for a variety of risks, CBC recommends the MTA:

Implement additional operating efficiencies in collaboration with labor by modifying work rules, increasing employee availability, and sharing the benefits of additional operational flexibility and higher productivity to cut costs while supporting competitive wages;  
Continue to reduce fare evasion, which is on track to cost more than $900 million this year, by:
Assessing the cost-effectiveness of enforcement efforts;
Refining the multifaceted approach to reducing fare evasion;
Accelerating the rollout of new faregates and a proof-of-payment system; and  
Working with the District Attorneys to actively pursue and try serial fare evasion cases;
Collaborate with the City to improve uptake, assess the cost-effectiveness, and consider expanding Fair Fares NYC;
Reduce paratransit per-trip expenses by;  
Using the paratransit fleet and broker services strategically to ensure users are provided with the lowest-cost transportation option that meets their needs; and  
Consider greater use of shared paratransit rides to reduce per-trip costs; and
Continue biennial fare and toll increases and consider tying them to year-over-year expense growth instead of a flat 4 percent.21
 
Conclusion 

Long-term stability of the MTA’s operating budget is essential to providing reliable transit services that support the region’s economy and quality of life. The MTA’s November 2025 Financial Plan projects manageable budget gaps, growing from $160 million in 2027 to $306 million in 2029, but CBC finds that the reported gaps mask the underlying difference between the MTA’s recurring revenues and recurring expenses. This structural budget gap is $1.1 billion in 2029, and the MTA may face deficits of $1 billion or more as early as 2030. Plus, there are many risks that could increase planned spending or projected revenue.

Smart choices now can align the MTA’s operating expenses and resources before more drastic action is needed. CBC recommends the MTA work with labor to further increase efficiency, control paratransit cost growth, reduce reliance on operating-budget-backed debt, and aggressively fight fare evasion. If necessary, the MTA should consider fare and toll increases that reflect actual expense growth—all in the service of keeping New York’s crucial public transportation network functioning well.
