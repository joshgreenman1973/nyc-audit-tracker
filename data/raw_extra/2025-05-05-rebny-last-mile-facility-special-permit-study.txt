Context 
Special Permit Overview 
The New York City Department of City Planning (DCP) is studying the creation of a special permit for last-
mile distribution facilities pursuant to commitments made by the Adams Administration to the City 
Council. DCP released a Draft Scope of Work for Environmental Review (DSOW)1 in March 2025, which 
describes an official use definition of a “parcel delivery facility” and provisions for the special permit.  
PROPOSED “PARCEL DELIVERY FACILITY” DEFINITION 
• 
Primarily used for unloading and sorting pre-packed goods and reloading those goods onto 
vehicles certified as Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) Vehicle Weight Classes 1-5 for final 
delivery 
• 
Has little to no activity related to extended storage, picking and packing, wholesale distribution, 
or retail 
SPECIAL PERMIT PROVISIONS 
• 
Applies to new parcel delivery facilities located in M, MX, and C8 districts 
• 
Applies to new development, change of use, and conversion projects 
• 
Exemptions are applied to:  
o 
Facilities with less than 50,000 SF of floor area 
o 
Water-dependent facilities, or warehouses that ship or receive materials or products by 
water 
o 
Facilities located more than 500 feet from zoning districts that permit residential use (R, 
C1-C6, and MX) and meet certain electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure standards 
specified by DCP 
o 
Facilities located on airport land or property owned/leased by the United States Postal 
Service (USPS) 
Study Purpose 
The special permit is likely to serve as a deterrent to future development of last-mile facilities and 
conversion of existing warehouses to last-mile use. Pursuing a special permit requires undergoing an 
extensive public review process without a guaranteed outcome; the timeline and entitlement risk would 
make the special permit very unattractive for developers and property owners, as indicated by 
stakeholder interviews conducted for this study. 
The special permit for hotels in M1 (light manufacturing) districts created in 2018 demonstrates the 
likelihood of this impact. Since the implementation of that regulation, no M1 hotel special permits have 
been approved. In other words, the special permit has served to eliminate all new hotel development in 
M1 districts to date.2

HR&A Advisors (HR&A) conducted this study on behalf of the Real Estate Board of New York (REBNY) to 
understand the potential impacts of the last-mile facility special permit on New York City. This study seeks 
to answer the following questions: 
1. What are recent market trends in the development of last-mile facilities in New York City? 
2. What has been the impact of the growth of the last-mile sector in New York City? 
3. What would be the impacts of a special permit for last-mile facilities be on New York City, in terms 
of economic activity, tax revenues, the environment, and transportation? 
4. Are there alternative policy approaches that would more effectively advance New York City’s 
sustainability goals? 
The study concludes that a special permit will not effectively advance New York City’s sustainability goals 
and that alternative policies can better address these concerns. 
 
Last-Mile Development Trends 
E-Commerce Trends 
Last-mile facilities have grown in New York City in response to the increasing popularity of online 
shopping and the need for proximity to customers to meet expectations for rapid delivery of goods. 
 
Since 2000, households’ and businesses’ desire to shop online has grown rapidly. COVID-19 accelerated 
this trend, and online shopping has continued to steadily increase since the pandemic. In 2024, 16.2% of 
all retail spending occurred online, totaling $1.2 trillion in annual spending. Should current growth 
trends continue, in five years, approximately 23% of all retail spending will occur online, totaling $2.1 
trillion in annual spending.

Defining Last-Mile Facilities 
Given the substantial rise in e-commerce, last-mile facilities have grown in and around New York City in 
order to meet delivery demand. Much of the growth has been driven by industry standards for two-day, 
next-day, or even same-day delivery.4 However, despite growth in the sector, there is no agreement on a 
definition of “last-mile facilities,” even among the seven developers and operators HR&A spoke to.  
For this analysis, HR&A defined last-mile facilities based on building size and current or anticipated 
tenant. We included both new construction and renovations of existing buildings with the intent to lease 
to a last-mile tenant. This definition was based on DCP’s DSOW, interviews with developers and operators, 
and observations from site tours.5,6

According to interviews with last-mile developers active in New York City, developers began to pursue 
multi-story distribution projects (e.g., Bronx Logistics Center and Red Hook Distribution Center) during the 
COVID-19 pandemic in response to rapidly increasing demand for distribution centers. Development of 
multi-story buildings was used as a strategy to maximize use of limited large industrial sites in New York 
City. However, the success of multi-story distribution centers in New York City – a largely untested 
product with fewer than a dozen built examples across the country9 – has been mixed. Interviews suggest 
that tenants may be reluctant to lease upper floors of buildings because they are not accustomed to 
operating multi-story distribution centers, domestic truck drivers are unfamiliar with such facilities, and 
truck circulation can be more complex in this type of project. Those challenges, combined with the 
significant square footage of these projects – ranging from 350,000-1,300,000 SF – have contributed to the 
relatively slow absorption of these buildings. 
 
Moreover, leasing activity declined during this time. Research has determined that Amazon is likely the 
largest user of last-mile facilities in New York City today. As the predominant user of last-mile space, 
Amazon’s real estate decisions have had notable impacts on the market, which largely comprises 
speculative developments responding to trends in market demand. Amazon made significant investments 
in its last-mile delivery network in New York City during the early COVID-19 years to meet exponentially 
growing demand for same-day delivery services. This resulted in Amazon leasing a significant share of 
last-mile properties across New York City over the last decade; Amazon had zero distribution hubs in New 
York City 10 years ago. Now that Amazon has an established distribution network, its absorption of new 
last-mile space has slowed. While Amazon’s future plans are not known, we conclude that past last-mile 
development trends in New York City were largely driven by Amazon’s unprecedented leasing activity and 
are not necessarily indicators of the size of the future development pipeline.  
 
Economic Benefits of Last-Mile Facilities 
The growth in last-mile facilities throughout the city has resulted in considerable economic benefits, 
including building investment, property tax generation, and job creation.  
Prior Uses of Last-Mile Sites  
New York City’s industrial landscape includes a wide range of warehousing, manufacturing, and 
distribution uses, as well as less-active uses such as parking and storage. While businesses in these 
sectors are operating in a variety of facilities, a large share conduct business in older masonry structures 
that have not received investment in decades. This lack of investment is in part due to the thin margins of 
many industrial businesses.10  
 
In New York City’s industrial areas, the 26 last-mile facilities identified by HR&A have comprised the 
majority of all new construction and significant renovations activity in recent years. Many of these 
investments occurred on sites generating few jobs or property taxes: nearly 70% of recently built or 
renovated last-mile facilities were vacant or used for parking. The top three prior uses of sites were: 
 
• 
Underutilized (includes vacant, abandoned, and/or dilapidated sites) 
• 
Parking (includes surface parking and/or 3PL sites, which are primarily used to park fleet vehicles) 
• 
Distribution/warehousing (includes B2B and/or wholesale distribution sites)

Property Tax Impacts 
Last-mile facility developers typically make significant capital improvements to industrial sites in order to 
create state-of-the art facilities with efficient loading and circulation. HR&A compared the property tax 
revenue generated by last-mile properties developed or renovated in the past 10 years (“redevelopment 
scenario”) to the estimated property tax revenue that these sites would generate today had no last-mile 
investments been made (“base case”).  
 
The “base case” estimated property tax for FY 2023-2024 is based on the site’s historic property tax (taxes 
collected in the year prior to redevelopment) escalated by the annual increase in assessed value of Class 
4 industrial properties citywide (Factories, Warehouses, and Condo Warehouse/Industrial) through 2024. 
 
One example of the incremental value generated by conversion of industrial sites to last-mile facilities is 
46-06 57th Avenue in Maspeth, Queens. The previously vacant site along Newton Creek was acquired by 
FedEx and converted to a last-mile distribution facility in 2017. Absent redevelopment, we estimate the 
site would have generated $869,000 in estimated property tax in FY 2023-2024. However, following 
FedEx’s renovation, the property generated over $1.1 million in actual property tax in FY 2023-2024.

Employment Impacts 
Last-mile facilities support local jobs, often for individuals without college degrees, and provide a large 
range of employment opportunities for New York City residents at all education levels. If future last-mile 
facilities are developed in Northern New Jersey instead of New York City, the city will lose out on those job 
opportunities.

Transportation and Environment 
Our research determined that past last-mile development trends in New York City cannot necessarily be 
relied on to project future demand for additional last-mile space. However, to the extent that there is 
demand for additional space, we assume that the creation of a special permit for last-mile facilities will 
intensify development activity in areas surrounding New York City – particularly Northern New Jersey. 
Unfortunately, an increase in last-mile facilities in New Jersey serving New York City could have significant 
pollution and traffic costs for residents and roadways across the region. Compared to a last-mile facility in 
New York City, a last-mile facility in New Jersey generates a much greater volume of traffic to reach 
consumers.

Sustainability Upgrades 
Last-mile operators and developers are becoming leaders in industrial building modernization and 
sustainability. Development of last-mile facilities has resulted in brownfield cleanup, building 
modernization and energy efficiency, green energy investments, improved truck queuing that is less 
disruptive to surrounding neighborhoods, and stormwater management that would not otherwise occur. 
Disincentivizing these investments would run counter to the environmental goals of the special permit. 13  
 
Of the 26 last-mile properties HR&A analyzed, three were formerly brownfield sites and underwent 
remediation. These sites include 68 Ferris Street in Brooklyn, 2980 Review Avenue in Queens, and 1 
Nassau Place in Staten Island. The Brooklyn and Queens sites were remediated through the Brownfield 
Cleanup Program, while the Staten Island site underwent the Voluntary Cleanup Program. These 
investments remediate contamination that would otherwise remain, delivering lasting benefits to the 
environment, local workers, pedestrians, and residents.

Policy Concerns and Implications 
A special permit is unlikely to effectively advance New York City’s sustainability goals and could have 
unintended consequences.  Other policy approaches should be explored.  
Lack of Impact on Existing Facilities 
The operations of the 26 identified existing facilities that have been built as-of-right in New York 
City over the past decade – the source of concerns that have led to the proposed special permit – 
will not be impacted by the special permit at all. The proposal for the special permit was generated 
because the existing facilities have caused concern among councilmembers and advocacy groups. 
However, the special permit would likely only be required for new construction of last-mile facilities or a 
change of use of an existing building to a last-mile facility, and will not have an impact on how existing 
facilities operate. 
 
Other policy approaches would much better satisfy the stated goals of the special permit, as described 
below.  
Unintended Consequences 
A special permit could not only be ineffective at hindering negative impacts of existing last-mile 
facilities but could also have unintended consequences. From an environmental perspective, if the 
special permit results in the intensification of last-mile development in northern New Jersey, this would 
result in an increase in delivery and commuting trips, VMTs, and emissions, with negative impact on both 
New York City and the region. Environmentally beneficial investments in New York City industrial sites 
associated with many last-mile facilities – such as brownfield cleanup, stormwater management, and EV 
charging – would be foregone. From an economic perspective, the city would lose out on tax revenues 
and employment if new facilities are located elsewhere.

Further, if triggered by significant renovations and/or change in occupancy, a special permit could 
significantly disincentivize major upgrades to existing facilities that improve environmental or traffic 
conditions. A special permit, lastly, could be difficult to enforce and constrain the ability of other goods-
moving industrial businesses to invest in their facilities given the difficulty of defining last-mile. 
Inflexibility of Land Use Policy 
Land use policy changes much less quickly than business technology and can quickly become 
outdated. 
 
The logistics industry is changing rapidly. Logistics operators shared that they are prioritizing improving 
efficiency, often through innovations in technology and operations.14 The last-mile sector has been 
advancing innovative technology for the past decade, including drone delivery systems, AI-powered 
technology to improve the efficiency of fulfillment centers, and electric delivery vehicles.15 These 
technological improvements will likely change the demand for, scale, and impact of last-mile facilities in 
ways we cannot predict today. A special permit for last-mile facilities may quickly become out of date as 
delivery models evolve.

often does not stay apace of business trends.

Policy Alternatives 
Ultimately, alternative transportation and enforcement policies can better target the concerns relating to 
truck traffic and emissions.  
• 
New York City can continue its ongoing efforts to embrace creative approaches to moving freight, 
including promotion of greener freight solutions, such as microhubs, e-cargo bikes, package 
lockers, and Blue Highways – the latter currently promoting usage of the city’s waterways to move 
freight to relieve congestion on city streets.16  
• 
The City and State are actively incentivizing EV truck fleets and can continue to do so, devoting 
additional resources and funding to accelerate sustainability investments. NYSERDA’s Truck 
Voucher Incentive program, funded at $2.3M in 2023, is an example of a state program that could 
receive more support to successfully stimulate green investments. At the local level, New York 
City’s Clean Trucks Program, which currently funds up to $215,000 per green truck replacement 
for businesses located in industrial business zones (IBZs), could be expanded to be available to all 
New York City businesses that operate truck fleets.17,18  
• 
Improving enforcement for quality of life violations could disincentivize last-mile operators from 
disturbing their neighbors. Operators shared that traffic and parking tickets are currently 
insufficient to deter disruptive behavior from last-mile operators. UPS noted that its drivers 
receive more tickets in New York City than in the rest of the country combined, but this has not 
resulted in business practice changes.19 Stimulating better compliance with existing laws and 
policies could generate more of the change desired by opponents to last-mile facilities.
