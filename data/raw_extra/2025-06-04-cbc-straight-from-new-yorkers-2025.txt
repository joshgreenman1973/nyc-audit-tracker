Executive Summary

New York City is at a critical juncture. Affordability challenges, economic shifts, and concerns over quality of life, public services, and safety are affecting the city's ability to attract and retain residents. 

Straight From New Yorkers 2025—the Citizens Budget Commission’s (CBC’s) updated NYC Resident Survey—provides a revealing portrait of how New Yorkers feel about their quality of life, public safety, government services, and future in the city. This survey’s third iteration, based on over 1,700 responses, highlights residents’ concerns and offers insights into the diverse perspectives of New Yorkers by income, race, ethnicity, and borough.

New Yorkers’ ratings of quality of life, quality of services, and public safety mostly remain well below pre-pandemic levels reported in 2017 but with modest improvements since 2023. This feedback highlights the urgent need for decisive leadership, targeted policies, and smart fiscal choices that strengthen public services while maintaining long-term financial stability. Ensuring New York’s competitiveness demands nothing less.

Straight from New Yorkers reveals:

Only 34 percent of New Yorkers rate the city's quality of life excellent or good in 2025, a substantial decrease from 51 percent in 2017;
The proportion of residents who rate their neighborhood an excellent or good place to live fell from 63 percent in 2017 to 56 percent in 2025, although the rating improved significantly since 2023;
Only 42 percent of New Yorkers rate public safety in their neighborhood excellent or good, down from 50 percent in 2017, although the rating improved since 2023. Still, the share of residents who feel very safe or safe walking alone in their neighborhood at night dropped from 70 percent in 2017 to 59 percent in 2025;
Subway safety ratings are alarmingly low, despite decreases in serious felony crimes: only 50 percent of New Yorkers feel safe during the day and only 22 percent feel safe at night, down substantially from 2017;
Only 27 percent of residents rate government services excellent or good, substantially down from 44 percent in 2017;
Most New Yorkers rate government services inadequate with the majority rating 23 out of 33 services fair or poor;
Satisfaction is especially low for housing services, homeless services, and mental health care; and
Only 11 percent rate government spending excellent or good, down from 21 percent in 2017.

There are important bright spots:

Some key quality of life indicators, such as neighborhood parks and ease of travel within the city, have rebounded to or above 2017 levels; and
Neighborhood services like libraries, fire protection, and EMS maintain relatively higher satisfaction, particularly in Manhattan and Staten Island.

Higher income households’ quality of life and neighborhood ratings decreased sharply from 2023 to 2025:

Income-based differences in satisfaction have diminished, which is largely due to the continued—and mostly unique—satisfaction slide among higher earners making over $100,000. 

New Yorkers’ ratings sometimes varied significantly by race, ethnicity, and borough:

Bronx residents consistently report the lowest satisfaction nearly across the board, including city quality of life, safety during the day or night, and all neighborhood services; and
Black and Hispanic New Yorkers rate both citywide and neighborhood services lower than White and Asian New Yorkers.
Fifty-five percent of New Yorkers plan to remain in the city over the next five years—down slightly from 2017;
The top three reasons New Yorkers cite for considering moving out of New York City are affordability (76 percent), safety (73 percent), and living space (60 percent); and
New Yorkers want the City administration to focus on safety, housing, infrastructure, and cleanliness.

New Yorkers reveal their priorities and factors they consider when deciding to stay in New York City or leave:

Straight from New Yorkers 2025 makes clear that city residents continue to be dissatisfied with many facets of city life and services. The city's post-pandemic satisfaction recovery is nowhere near complete. 

While some important areas have recently improved, persistent dissatisfaction with safety, services, and affordability—especially in the Bronx and among Black and Hispanic New Yorkers—signals where the City should target improvement efforts, policies, programs, and resources. 

Ensuring public safety, improving services, and enhancing quality of life requires smart fiscal, management, and investment choices, as CBC identified in its recently released Smart Choices, Better Future: Mayoral Platform for New York City. The findings stress the importance of responsiveness and a recommitment to the city’s foundational promise: a safe, livable, and opportunity-rich environment for all New Yorkers.

Introduction

New York City is at a critical juncture. Affordability challenges, the evolving economy, and concerns about the quality of life, public services, and public safety threaten New York’s ability to attract and retain residents. 

To better understand how New Yorkers rate quality of life and government services, the Citizens Budget Commission fielded its third iteration of the NYC Resident Survey during March and April of 2025.1 While just 16 months after the 2023 NYC Resident Survey, it is important to understand how ratings may have changed as the pandemic is further in the rear-view mirror.2 More than 1,700 New Yorkers responded, rating the city’s quality of life, public safety, and government services. They also shared whether they plan to stay in New York City over the next five years, what factors influence their decision to leave, and the priorities City leaders should focus on. 

CBC conducted the survey previously in 2017 and 2023 using the same survey research firm and employing the same methodology and consistent survey questions, allowing for robust comparisons over time. New York City conducted the survey in 2008.

In 2025, New Yorkers’ rate quality of life, government services, and public safety largely below the 2017 pre-pandemic baseline, with modest improvement since the 2023 survey 16 months prior. Now, 34 percent of New Yorkers rate quality of life in the city excellent or good, down from 51 percent in 2017. While 56 percent report their neighborhood as an excellent or good place to live, this is down from 63 percent in 2017.  Only 27 percent of residents rate government services excellent or good, substantially down from 44 percent in 2017.

New Yorkers rate a handful of quality-of-life indicators, such as neighborhood parks and ease of travel within the city, as highly as they did in 2017. They also highly rate library, fire, and emergency medical services.

The low subway safety ratings remain stagnant and well below 2017, despite a decline in subway crime since 2017, albeit with fewer passengers. Furthermore, ratings on many government services were low, with the majority of New Yorkers rating 23 of 33 services as fair or poor.

There are notable differences by race/ethnicity and location. Bronx residents are the least satisfied, often by a large margin. Black and Hispanic New Yorkers are less positive in their assessment of the city than Asian and White New Yorkers. 

The higher satisfaction levels among households earning over $100,000 seen in 2017 have dissipated, and New Yorkers across all incomes have remarkably similar ratings in 2025.

This report dives into the results in more depth, offering critical insights into New York City’s sputtering post-pandemic satisfaction recovery and the path forward to ensure an attractive and vibrant city that continues to be a magnet for residents and businesses.

New Yorkers Remain Dissatisfied with Quality of Life in the City

In 2025, only 34 percent of residents rank the quality of life in New York City excellent or good, down from 51 percent in 2017. New Yorkers continue to be more positive about their neighborhoods as a place to live than the city overall. However, Black and Hispanic New Yorkers and Bronx residents are more dissatisfied with the quality of life in their neighborhoods. 

Ratings on some quality-of-life indicators, such as neighborhood parks and playgrounds, have recovered to pre-pandemic levels, but many remain depressed.  

Only One in Three New Yorkers Rate Quality of Life as Excellent or Good

In 2025, just one out of every three New Yorkers rate the quality of life in New York City as excellent or good. (See Figure 1.)  This is a 33 percent decrease from the 51 percent who rated New York City quality of life as excellent or good in 2017. One in four rate it as poor. Furthermore, there was only a modest four-percentage-point improvement since 2023.

All Incomes, Races, Ethnicities, and Boroughs Rate Quality of Life Low

Ratings of quality of life in New York City are low across demographic groups. (See Figure 2.) Less than half of every group rates quality of life excellent or good. Still there is some variation, especially higher satisfaction among White New Yorkers and Manhattanites.

Forty percent of White New Yorkers positively rate quality of life, which is 12 percentage points higher than Hispanic New Yorkers (28 percent). Black (31 percent) and Asian New Yorkers (33 percent) rate quality of life just a few ticks higher than Hispanic New Yorkers.

Manhattan residents were the most satisfied, with 47 percent rating quality of life excellent or good. Only 24 percent of Bronx residents rate quality of life excellent or good. Brooklyn, Queens, and Staten Island residents are all within a couple of percentage points of each other and only modestly higher than the Bronx residents. 

In 2025, New Yorkers at all income levels feel similarly about quality of life in the city, with 35 percent of both the lowest income residents earning $0-$35,000 and the highest income residents earning $200,000 or more rating quality of life as excellent or good.

New Yorkers’ Priorities for City Government: Safety, Housing, Infrastructure and Cleanliness

Most New Yorkers (54 percent) say that safety is one of the three most important issues requiring more government attention, nearly double the 30 percent in 2017, but comparable to 2023. 

The second most frequently cited issue is housing, including housing affordability, identified by around one-third of residents, which is slightly higher than in 2017 and similar to 2023. One in four residents mentions infrastructure, down from 30 percent in 2017 but up from16 percent in 2023. Cleanliness of the city is a priority issue for 22 percent of residents, up from 16 percent in 2017. One in five say homelessness is a top priority, similar to 2017 and a few percentage points lower than 2023.

Just 9 percent said that schools were a priority issue in both 2025 and 2023, down from 19 percent in 2017. Seventeen percent cite affordability, excluding housing affordability, up from 13 percent in 2017. Immigration, which was cited by 16 percent of residents in 2023, lost prominence and is referenced by less than 7 percent of residents in 2025. This is still higher than the 2 percent who mentioned immigration in 2017.

More Positive About Neighborhoods but Satisfaction Remains Below Pre-Pandemic Levels

Overall, 56 percent of New Yorkers say their neighborhood is an excellent or good place to live. (See Figure 3.) This is 11 percent lower than 2017, when 63 percent rated their neighborhood as an excellent or good place to live. However, roughly half of the 13-percentage-point drop from 2017 to 2023 has been recovered. 

New Yorkers’ feelings about their neighborhoods are both more positive and more consistent over time than their feelings about the city as a whole. In 2017, there was an 11-point satisfaction gap between the share rating their neighborhood positively and the share rating city quality of life positively. This satisfaction gap more than doubles to 22 points in 2025, driven more by persistent dissatisfaction with the city as a whole.  

Bronx Residents, Black and Hispanic New Yorkers Most Dissatisfied with Their Neighborhoods as Places to Live

New Yorkers across income groups largely rate satisfaction with their neighborhoods as places to live the same, with more than half calling them excellent or good. (See Figure 4.) Of those earning between $35,000 and $50,000, 52 percent rate their neighborhoods excellent or good, compared to 58 percent for those earning more than $200,000. In fact, just one percentage point separated the bottom and top income bands. 

However, there was stark variation by geography. Residents of the Bronx are much more dissatisfied with their neighborhoods as places to live. Just 36 percent of Bronx residents say their neighborhood is excellent or good, compared to 57 percent in Brooklyn and Queens, 65 percent in Manhattan and 69 percent in Staten Island. This substantial 21 to 33 percentage-point-gap is a stark statement of dissatisfaction in the Bronx.

Neighborhood ratings also vary by race and ethnicity. Only 42 percent of Black and 46 percent of Hispanic New Yorkers say their neighborhood is excellent or good, significantly below the 57 percent of Asian and 70 percent of White New Yorkers.

Higher Income Households’ Quality of Life and Neighborhood Ratings Decreased Sharply in 2025 

In 2017, higher income New Yorkers rated their city and neighborhood quality of life much more positively than other residents. But that gap has been wiped out in 2025 because ratings by New Yorkers earning more than $100,000 decreased sharply, continuing the decline in 2023. (See Figure 5.)

In 2017, 59 percent of New Yorkers earning between $100,000 and $200,000 and 69 percent of those earning over $200,000 rated the city quality of life excellent or good. By 2025, those numbers drop sharply to 32 percent and 35 percent—a decline of nearly 50 percent. The ratings by households earning less than $100,000 also declined, but much less significantly.

Neighborhood ratings fell as well. The share of people earning between $100,000 and $200,000 rating their neighborhood excellent or good fell 24 percent, from 73 percent in 2017 to 55 percent in 2025. For those earning more than $200,000, the drop was 34 percent, from 85 percent in 2017 to 58 percent in 2025. 

Important Bright Spots—Neighborhood Parks and Cultural Activities

New Yorkers’ ratings of neighborhood parks and the availability of cultural activities have fully recovered to pre-pandemic levels. (See Figure 6.) Fifty-six percent of New Yorkers now rate neighborhood parks and playgrounds excellent or good, compared to 57 percent in 2017. Furthermore, 50 percent rate the availability of cultural activities in their neighborhoods excellent or good in 2025, which is now even higher than the 48 percent in 2017. 

Cleanliness and Noise Remain a Challenge, Despite Recent Rating Improvement

In 2025, excellent or good ratings for neighborhood cleanliness (36 percent), control of street noise (34 percent), and air quality (29 percent) have not fully rebounded. These ratings are 16 percent to 23 percent lower than the 2017 pre-pandemic ratings. Still, by 2025, ratings on street noise and air quality have recovered approximately 45 percent of the 2017 to 2023 loss, while neighborhood cleanliness has only recovered 15 percent of the 2017 to 2023 loss.

Manhattan Residents Drive Recovery in Traffic and Ease of Travel Ratings 

While ratings of traffic and ease of travel are back to pre-pandemic levels, these categories are still not viewed positively by the majority of New Yorkers. In 2025, 36 percent of New Yorkers rate overall ease of travel excellent or good, compared to 37 percent in 2017 and 30 percent in 2023. The percent rating traffic excellent or good was also within 1 percentage point of the 2017 level, however, this was a very low positive rating of just 19 percent in 2025 and had dipped to 13 percent in 2023. 

The 2025 survey was fielded just two to three months after the launch of congestion pricing in Manhattan’s central business district. While citywide ratings are similar in 2017 and 2025, there is notable borough variation for these metrics. (See Figure 7.) 

In 2025, the share of New Yorkers rating ease of travel and traffic excellent or good increases in Manhattan and Staten Island and decreases in the Bronx. Nearly half of Manhattanites rate ease of travel excellent or good in 2025, up from 43 percent in 2017 and 39 percent in 2023.  For Manhattan residents, positive ratings for traffic increase to 24 percent from a low of 12 percent in 2023. The portion that rate traffic excellent or good in Staten Island increases 9 percentage points to 21 percent. 

Conversely, residents of the Bronx are less positive in 2025. Just 24 percent rate ease of travel excellent or good, down 31 percent from 2017 and even lower than the 27 percent who rated it excellent or good in 2023.  

Public Safety Ratings Remain Below Pre-Pandemic Levels, Despite Some Recent Crime Reductions

New York City crime rates spiked during the pandemic and remained elevated through 2023, contributing to a significant drop in how New Yorkers rated public safetyduring the day, at night, and on the subway. Since then, many categories of crime have declined with the exception of felony assaults, which remain higher than in 2017. 

In 2025, 42 percent of New Yorkers rated public safety in their neighborhood excellent or good, up from 37 percent in 2023 but still significantly below 50 percent in 2017. People generally feel safer walking alone in their neighborhoods at night and in parks during the day, but overall public safety ratings are lowest for residents of the Bronx,and Hispanic and Black New Yorkers. 

Despite lower levels of subway crime and an increased police presence, New Yorkers continue to feel unsafe on the subway with Staten Island residents and Asian New Yorkers reporting the lowest subway safety ratings.  

Many Categories of Crime Declining Since 2023, But Felony Assault Remains Elevated

Post-pandemic crime levels peaked in 2023, with all seven major felony crimes greater than in 2017.3 (See Table 1.) There was a 2 percent decrease in the seven major felony crimes from 2023 to 2024, and through May 18, 2025, serious crimes have fallen another 6 percent, compared to the same period last year.4 Despite these declines, crime in all seven categories remains above 2017 levels.

Citywide Patterns Compared to 2017

Murders declined 2 percent in 2024 and, so far in 2025, are 29 percent below the same period last year. Robbery, burglary, grand larceny, and grand larceny auto also declined in 2024 and so far in 2025. However, felony assaults increased 6 percent in 2024 and were 47 percent higher than 2017. Felony assaults have been flat so far this year. Rape has increased, but New York State broadened its definition, with the new standard taking effect on September 1, 2024, so the trend is difficult to interpret.  

Transit System Crime

Serious felony crimes in the city’s subway system declined 3 percent during the first four months of 2025, compared to the first four months of 2024.5 With fewer crimes and more riders, crimes per subway rider declined by 5 percent over this period.6

In 2017, there were more serious felony crimes on subways during the first four months of the year, but there were also more riders. Total complaints in the transit system—bus and subway—have been fairly stable, with a 1 percent increase in the first quarter of 2025 from 1,204 to 1,217.7

Public Safety Ratings Well Below Pre-Pandemic Levels

In 2025, 42 percent of New Yorkers rate public safety in their neighborhood excellent or good. While this is down from 50 percent in 2017, it marks a partial recovery from the low of 37 percent in 2023. (See Figure 8.) About 77 percent feel very safe or somewhat safe in a park during the day, a 10 percent decline since 2017, and more than half (59 percent) feel safe walking alone in their neighborhood at night, a 16 percent decline. 

Subway Public Safety Ratings Remain Far Below Pre-Pandemic Levels with No Improvement Since 2023

New Yorkers continue to feel far less safe on the subway in 2025 than in 2017. (See Figure 9.) Roughly 51 percent of New Yorkers now feel somewhat or very safe on the subway during the day, down from 82 percent in 2017. Just 22 percent of New Yorkers feel somewhat or very safe on the subway at night, less than half the 46 percent who did in 2017. In fact, half of New Yorkers report they feel very unsafe on subways at night. Despite increased police presence and lower crime, these levels are the same as 2023.

Safety Ratings Vary by Race, Ethnicity, and Borough, but Not by Income

Bronx residents are the least likely to feel very or somewhat safe in the city, and their ratings of daytime safety in a park decreased substantially since 2017. (See Figure 10A.) Only 53 percent of Bronx residents feel very or somewhat safe in parks or playgrounds during the day—26 to 30 percentage points lower than all other boroughs and a 28 percent decrease from 2017. (See Figure A1 in the Appendix.) 

The gap between the Bronx and other boroughs is narrower for neighborhood safety at night at 15 to 21 percentage points. The gap between day and night safety ratings for Bronx residents was 9 percentage points, while there was a bigger swing of 18 percentage points for all other boroughs from day to night.

Bronx residents also feel very unsafe on the subway during day or night, as do Staten Islanders. Residents of both boroughs reported significantly lower levels of daytime subway safety in 2025, compared to 2017. (See Figure A2 in the Appendix.) 

In general, Hispanic and Black New Yorkers rate safety lower than others. While Asian New Yorkers feel safe on subways during the day (63 percent), they are the least likely to feel safe on the subway at night (17 percent). White New Yorkers continue to report the highest levels of safety. 

As with other measures, ratings among New Yorkers with different incomes converged in 2025. In 2017, safety ratings generally increased with income level, but this is no longer the case as rating declines in 2025 are generally greater among higher income households. Now, safety ratings across income bands vary very little. 

New Yorkers Generally Dissatisfied with City Government and Services

New Yorkers generally rate New York City government activities and services negatively. In 2025, just 27 percent rate government services overall excellent or good, a substantial decrease from 44 percent in 2017. While there are a few bright spots—including ease of travel, libraries, and garbage collection—the majority of New Yorkers rate 23 of the 33 government activities or services fair or poor. Still, government satisfaction remains well below 2017 for many measures despite some improvement in the past 16 months. 

Geographically, Bronx residents are the least satisfied with neighborhood services, while residents of Manhattan and Staten Island are the most satisfied. Black and Hispanic residents are the least satisfied with neighborhood services, while White New Yorkers are the most satisfied. 

New Yorkers’ Post-Pandemic Low Ratings of City Government Activities Have Not Budged

In 2025, only 27 percent of New Yorkers rate the quality of New York City government services excellent or good, down from 44 percent in 2017. (See Figure 11.) 

Coming out of the COVID pandemic, less than half of New Yorkers feel the government prepares the city for an emergency, nearly half the rate of 2017. Only one in four rate the government’s promotion of economic growth excellent or good. Only one in ten New Yorkers rate the City government’s spending of tax dollars wisely as excellent or good. Similarly, only one in ten rate how the government addresses New Yorkers’ housing needs excellent or good. The highest positive rating is for protecting the city from a terrorist attack, which is still 35 percent lower than in 2017.

New Yorkers Report Low to Middling Satisfaction with Many Citywide Services 

In general, New Yorkers rate City services negatively with most residents rating 10 of 12 services fair or poor. Ratings on a handful of the services have recovered to 2017 levels, but, in many cases, residents were dissatisfied with services to begin with. (See Figure 12.)

On the positive side, around half of New Yorkers rate 311 and NYC.gov excellent or good. However, this share is 20 percent and 28 percent below 2017, respectively.

New Yorkers’ current ratings of public schools and after school programs are comparable to 2017—44 percent and 40 percent rate them excellent or good. Summer youth programs, which were not on the 2017 survey, rate excellent or good by 41 percent of New Yorkers, up from 35 percent in 2023. 

Generally, residents rate social services poorly. Fewer than one in four New Yorkers rate children’s services, services for homeless people, and public housing excellent or good, which is now in line with 2017 levels, but remains very low. 

No pre-pandemic baseline rating exists for some City services that were added to the survey in 2023. Some New Yorkers are positive about public assistance, with 44 percent rating it excellent or good. Health services are positively rated by 41 percent, but only 22 percent rate mental health and substance use services excellent or good.

Residents Satisfied with Some Neighborhood Services, like Libraries, Fire Protection, Household Garbage Pickup, and Emergency Medical Services

In 2025, the share of New Yorkers rating fire protection, public libraries, and emergency medical services excellent or good exceeds 65 percent, which is within three percentage points of the 2017 rating. (See Figure 13.) Garbage and recycling collection have also rebounded and are rated positively by more than 60 percent of respondents. 

Conversely, there has been less of a post-pandemic recovery for lower rated services.  Forty-four percent of New Yorkers rate pedestrian safety excellent or good, down from 53 percent in 2017. The share of New Yorkers rating bike safety excellent or good dropped 10 percentage points from 2017, while parking enforcement dropped 12 percentage points. 

Furthermore, the share of New Yorkers rating transit services excellent or good has not moved in the past 16 months, at 51 percent for bus and 44 percent for subway. 

Bronx Residents Least Satisfied with Neighborhood Services; Manhattan and Staten Island Most Satisfied

Bronx residents (shown in yellow on Figure 14) rate neighborhood services lower than any other borough. The share of Bronx residents rating any service excellent or good does not exceed 60 percent. 

For some services, the gap between the Bronx and other boroughs is particularly large.  For example, only 48 percent of Bronx residents rate libraries excellent or good, while the next lowest boroughs—Brooklyn and Staten Island—are 18 percentage points higher. 

Manhattanites and Staten Islanders, on the other hand, generally rate neighborhood services the highest. Manhattan is consistently at or near the top, except for pedestrian safety and bike safety. Staten Islanders, while often highly satisfied, note dissatisfaction with libraries, subways, and the condition of streets and roads. 

Satisfaction with Neighborhood Services Lowest Among Black and Hispanic Residents

While ratings of neighborhood services vary less by race and ethnicity than by geography, Black and Hispanic residents report much lower levels of satisfaction. (See Figure 15.) Black and Hispanic New Yorkers give similar ratings for some services, such as fire protection, rat control, and parking enforcement. But Black New Yorkers are the least satisfied with public libraries, bus services, and stormwater drainage and sewer maintenance and at least 7 percentage points lower than Hispanic residents. 

White and Asian residents are more satisfied, with a majority rating 9 of the 15 neighborhood services excellent or good. 

Migration: Will They Stay or Will They Go?

Nearly 55 percent of New Yorkers plan to still live in the city five years from now, close to the 58 percent who planned to stay in 2017. The top six factors influencing New Yorkers’ decisions to leave permanently are: housing affordability, crime/safety, living space, taxes, amenities, and job opportunities. Education and childcare are important factors for households with young children.

Majority of New Yorkers Say They Will Stay in NYC in the Next 5 Years, But Still Lower than 2017

Nearly 55 percent of New Yorkers report they plan to be in New York City for the next five years, three percentage points lower than in 2017. (See Figure 16.) Nineteen percent plan to leave within five years, roughly the same as in 2017, but down from 25 percent in 2023. More than one-quarter do not know if the plan to stay. 

In 2025, Black, White, and Hispanic New Yorkers are more likely to plan to stay than in 2023, but only Black New Yorkers’ share exceeds its 2017 level. (See Figure 17.) Asian New Yorkers, on the other hand, are less likely to plan to stay in 2025 (55 percent) than 2017 (64 percent).

Fewer working-age New Yorkers plan to stay compared to residents 65 years of age and older, which is similar to 2017. (See Figure 18.) Around half of New Yorkers age 35 to 54 years old say they will stay over the next 5 years, while 67 percent of those 75 years or older plan to stay. The only group more likely to stay in 2025 than in 2017 are New Yorkers ages 25 to 34 years old. Concerningly, just 50 percent of New Yorkers between 18 and 24 years of age plan to stay in the city, down from 60 percent in 2017. 

Primary Relocation Considerations Are Affordability, Safety, and Living Space 

When asked how important 13 identified factors are in the decision to permanently leave New York City, the largest share of New Yorkers rate affordability (76 percent) and safety (73 percent) as very important. (See Figure 19.) 

Sixty percent of New Yorkers say living space is very important when making a decision to leave, followed by taxes (55 percent), job opportunities (51 percent), retirement (50 percent), and amenities (47 percent).

These results reinforce the importance of New York City’s value proposition and ensuring the benefits of living here—cultural and social amenities, and economic opportunity—exceed the challenges of high costs, small apartments, and quality of life.

While schools (35 percent) and childcare (29 percent) rate lower as very important factors overall, households with children rate these factors as more important, especially families with children under 13 years old. (See Figure 20.) Among households with children, 65 percent rate schools as very important and 50 percent rate childcare as very important. These shares are higher for families with children under 13, with 70 percent rating schools and 59 percent rating childcare as very important. Around one-quarter of households without children rate these as very important, while roughly another quarter say they do not know.

Conclusion

Straight from New Yorkers 2025 paints a stark picture: New Yorkers remain much more dissatisfied with the quality of life, City services, and public safety than they were before the pandemic. If New York City is to remain competitive and continue attracting residents and businesses, people have to feel much more positively about many of the aspects of their life in the city. The findings about Bronx residents and among Black and Hispanic New Yorkers, where dissatisfaction levels are the highest, are particularly troubling.  

Straight from New Yorkers 2025 sends an important message to the City’s leaders: urgent improvement is necessary. These findings should drive management focus, improvement efforts, and resources.

New York City has proven itself resilient again and again. But progress requires intentional, focused leadership and tangible action. Now is the time to deliver the meaningful improvements New York needs.

Appendix
Rigorous Survey Methodology Provides Sound Comparisons

The 2025 Resident Feedback Survey, administered for CBC by Polco, employed a random sample of nearly 49,500 New York City households, with responses from 1,750 New Yorkers (4 percent response rate).8 The survey was sent in two waves, with the second wave added to strengthen validity. Responses were gathered from March to April 2025. The citywide margin of error is +/- 2 percent. Given the large sample size, it also provides statistically valid results by race/ethnicity and income. The margin of error for results is between +/-5 percent and +/- 7 percent for race/ethnicity, and between +/-4 percent and +/- 7 percent for income. Borough results have a margin of error of between +/- 4 percent and +/- 8 percent. Unlike prior iterations, this wave was not large enough to report results by Community Board.

The survey is very comparable to CBC’s 2023 and 2017 NYC Resident Feedback Surveys, as well as to a similar survey conducted by the City of New York in 2008.9 The same survey firm was engaged to conduct all four surveys, using comparable methodology and materials. Most questions were replicated in all four surveys.
