EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
On January 31, 2019, the New York City Housing Authority 
(“NYCHA”) committed to implementing essential reforms to improve 
housing conditions for NYCHA residents through an agreement (the 
“HUD Agreement” or “Agreement”) with the U.S. Attorney’s Ofﬁce for 
the Southern District of New York (“SDNY”), the U.S. Department of 
Housing and Urban Development (“HUD”), and the City of New York 
(the “City”). As part of the terms, a monitor was appointed to oversee 
and evaluate adherence to the Agreement. After the First Monitor 
completed a ﬁve-year term, on February 28, 2024, the current co-
Monitors (“Monitors”), Neil Barofsky and Matthew Cipolla of Jenner & 
Block LLP (“Jenner”), began their ﬁve-year term. This is their eighth 
report. 
In their prior reports, available on the Monitors’ website, the 
Monitors detailed their assessment of NYCHA’s compliance with the 
HUD Agreement, including NYCHA’s successes and challenges. In 
this report, and others going forward, the Monitors provide regular 
updates on NYCHA’s progress in meeting those requirements. This 
report includes data for February 1, 2026 to April 30, 2026, unless 
otherwise noted.  
NYCHA has continued to build on the progress described in 
the Monitors’ previous reports and should be commended for those 
efforts, although several important areas for improvement remain, as 
detailed below and throughout this report. To fully comply with the 
HUD Agreement, NYCHA must have sufficient resources for both 
operational expenses and capital improvements, a concern the 
Monitors raised in their second annual report, and that remains no 
less urgent given the possibility of significant federal budget cuts in 
the coming year. 
The Monitors again recognize and thank those who are 
working to improve NYCHA and help it fully meet its obligations 
under the HUD Agreement, including the constructive and 
collaborative approach taken by NYCHA’s leaders and staff; the vital 
information received from, and leadership shown by, NYCHA 
residents; and the expertise and engagement from SDNY and HUD 
officials and other stakeholders.

obligations related to heat, elevators, pests and waste, lead, mold,

Organizational Change 
NYCHA committed under the HUD Agreement to change its 
management, organizational, and workforce structure in a manner to 
ensure sustained compliance with the Agreement’s requirements. 
That commitment is embodied in the Transformation Plan, which 
contains both broad principles for improvement and specific 
initiatives intended to improve NYCHA’s processes and performance. 
Further detail concerning these topics is included in Section II of this 
report. 
As detailed in Section II, NYCHA, in coordination with the 
Monitors, the Independent Data Analyst, HUD, and SDNY, has 
continued to build on the Mold and Leak Restore and Renew (“MLRR”) 
program, a targeted initiative focused on aged mold and leak 
conditions at NYCHA’s highest-need sites. Since launching in August 
2025, NYCHA has performed MLRR work at four developments and 
closed nearly 2,000 mold and leak work orders that had been open 
for over 100 days. NYCHA has made several efforts to iterate and 
improve the program, and the Monitors look forward to continuing to 
work with NYCHA to refine and strengthen its impact.  
NYCHA has also taken steps to improve its management of 
vendor contracts. In December 2025, NYCHA launched the Contract 
Management Initiative (“CMI”), a cross-functional group with 
representatives from across the authority, designed to identify gaps 
in contract management and leverage collective expertise to address 
them. As detailed in Section II, the CMI has completed its first project 
of defining NYCHA staff roles and responsibilities as they relate to

pipeline.  
NYCHA has also continued to improve its tracking of 
supervisory inspections of work orders closed by skilled trades

and is currently ahead of schedule on upgrades designed to enhance 
that capability. While NYCHA has not yet implemented tracking for 
supervisory inspections of maintenance workers, it projects that a 
solution will be in place by August—several months ahead of the 
previously anticipated timeline. 
Finally, as part of its Transformation Plan, NYCHA has

they are producing meaningful improvements in services to residents 
and sustained compliance with the HUD Agreement’s requirements. 
Capital Expenditures 
Since the Monitors’ April 2026 Report, the Monitors continue 
to track NYCHA’s management of City Capital Action Plan (“CCAP”)

CCAP funds from the City for fiscal year 2026, bringing the total 
funding under the Agreement to $1.6 billion. As of April 30, 2026, 
NYCHA has spent approximately $980 million and has contracts in 
place for an additional approximately $900 million. 1 The Monitors’ 
ongoing review of CCAP projects includes tracking project progress, 
conducting site visits, examining delays, evaluating the resulting 
operating impact, and regularly meeting with NYCHA’s Asset & 
Capital Management team.

I.1 
Heat 
The HUD Agreement requires NYCHA to improve heating 
conditions for NYCHA residents through a variety of measures, such 
as (1) replacing a specified number of boilers, (2) establishing 
systems for responding to apartments affected by in-apartment 
heating failures, (3) investigating failures to timely restore heat, (4) 
notifying residents of heating outages, (5) monitoring the 
temperature in apartments, and (6) reducing the duration and 
number of heating service interruptions experienced by residents.2 
The section below provides an update on NYCHA’s progress with 
respect to outstanding heating-related obligations under the 
Agreement for the first seven months of the 2025‒2026 heating 
season, namely from October 1, 2025 to April 30, 2026. The Monitors’ 
next report will provide a comprehensive assessment of NYCHA’s 
performance under the Agreement’s heating obligations for the 
entire 2025‒2026 heating season.3  
A. 
NYCHA’s Boiler Replacements (Requirement No. H1) 
The HUD Agreement requires NYCHA to “replace or address 
approximately 500 boilers by [the end of] 2026,” and it allows for 
some to be replaced by NYCHA and some to be replaced by PACT 
partners.4 Although NYCHA completed this obligation by December 
31, 2025,5 it has continued to replace boilers at NYCHA-managed 
developments in 2026, replacing 13 boilers since the beginning of 
the year. It plans to complete replacement of 92 additional boilers in 
2026.

“parent” work order, which contains multiple “child” 
work orders for each step of the remediation process—

F. 
Lead Abatement Vendor Oversight 
NYCHA relies on vendors to conduct the majority of its lead 
abatements. The Monitors continue to review NYCHA’s oversight of 
these vendors with the goal of improving compliance with regulatory 
requirements and maximizing the efficient use of its resources. As 
part of its responsibilities in administering its Lead Abatement 
Program, NYCHA procures and retains abatement vendors through a 
competitive process that requires compliance with applicable EPA, 
HUD, OSHA, New York State, and New York City requirements for 
lead-based paint activities. Vendors must hold appropriate firmwide 
certifications, and individual supervisors and workers must also 
maintain their own federal certifications and training credentials, 
which NYCHA verifies prior to assignment of work. NYCHA also 
reviews vendors’ compliance history, including prior performance on 
NYCHA projects and, where available, regulatory or enforcement 
history, before issuing work and periodically thereafter. 
Beyond prequalification, NYCHA maintains ongoing oversight 
of abatement vendor performance. NYCHA authorizes and initiates 
abatement work through formal Notices to Proceed and associated 
work orders that establish defined response times, mobilization 
requirements, work practices, and documentation obligations. 
NYCHA program management staff and field inspectors monitor 
compliance with containment procedures, supervision requirements, 
EPA notification protocols, and clearance documentation. Where 
deficiencies are identified (whether through field observation, 
compliance audits, or agency review), NYCHA issues written notices 
of non-compliance or Letters of Direction, requires corrective action 
plans, and may mandate retraining. Escalation measures can include 
suspension of work orders, withholding or reduction of payments, or 
contract termination.

HUD’s Guidance and Standards (Requirement Nos. I5 and

1. 
NSPIRE Inspections  
Under the HUD Agreement, NYCHA is required to maintain 
housing that is “functionally adequate, operable, and free of health 
and safety hazards” and make all repairs in accordance with HUD’s 
physical condition standards, NSPIRE.140 HUD regularly conducts 
inspections of NYCHA’s developments to determine NYCHA’s 
compliance with the NSPIRE standards. 141 NSPIRE inspections include 
a random sampling of apartments and interior and exterior common 
areas.  
Health and safety deficiencies identified are categorized as 
“life-threatening,” “severe,” “moderate,” or “low.” “Life-threatening” 
and “severe” are weighted more heavily and result in higher point 
deductions. To receive a passing score, a development must receive 
an overall score of 60 or greater and have fewer than 30 points 
deducted for in-unit deficiencies. If 30 or more points are deducted 
for such in-unit deficiencies, but the development otherwise scores a 
passing grade of 60 or above, that score will be adjusted down to 59, 
and the development will receive a failing grade. 
Between January 1, 2026, and April 30, 2026, NYCHA received 
NSPIRE results for five inspected developments. All five (100%) 
received passing scores. By comparison NYCHA received passing 
scores for 71.9% of 89 inspections conducted in 2025.  
2. 
60-Day Survey 
NYCHA must meet certain requirements to resolve possible 
issues in apartments that fail their NSPIRE inspections. The HUD 
Agreement requires NYCHA to complete a survey of the entire 
development for any development that scored less than 60 during its 
NSPIRE inspection. 142 This includes reinspecting all apartments, 
common areas, and exteriors for deficiencies. 143 Because of resource 
and capacity constraints, as well as the size of certain developments, 
NYCHA finds it to be a challenge to comply with this requirement 
within the 60-day period. To address this, HUD, NYCHA, and the 
Monitors created a protocol for seeking an extension of the 60-day 
survey requirement as a temporary measure until NYCHA can timely 
complete its HUD Agreement guidelines. The protocol requires

SECTION II: ORGANIZATIONAL CHANGE  
Under the HUD Agreement, NYCHA is required to make broad 
management and organizational reforms across the agency that will 
improve its overall functioning and its delivery of services to 
residents. As discussed in the Monitors’ prior reports, NYCHA’s plan 
for doing so is contained in its “Transformation Plan,” which lays out 
broad principles for improvement and core values for the 
organization—e.g., “creating a culture of service,” “strengthening 
NYCHA’s partnership with its residents,” and “using data to drive 
decision making”—as well as 47 specific initiatives intended to 
improve NYCHA’s processes and performance. Consistent with those 
broad principles, NYCHA also works with HUD, SDNY, and the 
Monitors to identify additional initiatives that will build on those 
specific projects to continue to improve itself as an organization.  
In this report, the Monitors provide updates on significant 
developments over the last quarter within key areas relating to 
NYCHA’s organizational change. Overall, NYCHA has continued to 
make progress on initiatives and systemic policy changes that are 
leading to significant improvements in resident services. It has 
devoted significant efforts and resources to the MLRR program, which 
has led to the closing of nearly 2,000 open mold and leak work orders 
that were open for over 100 days. It has launched a cross-department 
collaborative effort to improve its practices for overseeing and 
managing vendors that handle a significant amount of work for 
NYCHA. And it has made improvements in how it tracks inspections 
of the work done by its maintenance workers. NYCHA should 
continue its efforts in all these key areas, and the Monitors will be 
working with NYCHA to assess the impacts of all of its efforts 
undertaken pursuant to its Transformation Plan.

Highlights 
The MLRR program has had a meaningful impact in addressing 
mold and leak work orders at NYCHA. In approximately nine months, 
MLRR has closed 1,981 mold and leak work orders that had been 
open for over 100 days. In addition, through the MLRR program, 
NYCHA has addressed 1,297 additional work orders, including, most 
commonly, work such as pipe repair, stove repairs, brickwork, and 
painting. 
NYCHA’s work on MLRR is improving over time. In some 
instances, that impact is visible in a development’s ranking on the 
Consolidated Mold and Leaks Scorecard. For example, while 
Sotomayor’s Scorecard ranking did not materially change, Whitman’s 
improved substantially. Whitman ranked 111th among NYCHA 
developments before the program and ranked 72nd as of June

small portion of the metrics feeding that score. Moreover, an 
independent analysis conducted by the IDA highlights additional 
markers of progress. At Whitman, the number of open work orders

start to end of MLRR, with notable improvements also achieved at 
Ingersoll and Red Hook East. Across all MLRR developments to date, 
the average days to resolve MLRR mold and leak work orders 
declined 47%—from 424 days pre-MLRR to 225 days one month after

NYCHA has also consistently responded to feedback about the

orders. From a resident’s perspective, this avoids increasing the

and noted the potential benefit to NYCHA by avoiding access issues 
that may result in follow-up repairs. NYCHA agreed and has followed

NYCHA has also initiated reforms based on its own learned 
experience. For example, and as noted above, NYCHA has extended 
the concept of completing non-MLRR work beyond where MLRR staff 
are already in the apartment making MLRR-related repairs. Now, 
NYCHA also uses MLRR staff to conduct separate non-MLRR repairs 
during downtime from MLRR work, such as when an MLRR staff 
member cannot access any of the apartments they have scheduled 
for the day. In other words, rather than having a plumber wait to 
access a particular targeted apartment, NYCHA will deploy the 
plumber to fix a plumbing issue nearby, even if not at a targeted 
apartment, so that the plumber’s time is used more efficiently. This is 
reflected in the chart in the previous section in the column titled “Non-
MLRR Work Orders Closed in Non-MLRR Apartments by MLRR Staff.” 
This increases the program’s overall impact on a development and is 
a sensible way to make use of lost time resulting from access issues.  
NYCHA has also taken initiative to increase the total number of 
residents’ work orders being completed by working at multiple 
developments within a single phase. Running two developments in 
close succession has increased total output, as it allows NYCHA to 
make better use of the time its staff would otherwise spend waiting 
for apartment access. The data bears this out: the scope of initial 
MLRR work orders for Ingersoll and Red Hook East totaled 1,461 work 
orders—a 128% increase compared to Whitman, which had 641 work 
orders—and completed work orders increased by 97% over a

comparable program timeframe, from 557 at Whitman to 1,096 at

is readily available and access rates are high, work order closures

bottleneck and is working to increase that capacity. During MLRR at 
Ingersoll and Red Hook East, NYCHA observed that incomplete

repair team in such cases, NYCHA can reconnect plumbing 
immediately after new cabinets are installed, making repairs more 
efficiently and in a manner that better serves residents. Most recently, 
NYCHA has moved to hire a dedicated planner responsible for 
scheduling MLRR work around residents’ availability. This is a change 
that could meaningfully improve access issues and help further 
increase the efficiency of the program.

3. 
Continuous Improvement Opportunities 
Alongside the progress described above, the program has two 
key areas for additional improvements as NYCHA continues to strive 
toward NYCHA and the Monitors’ shared goals of significant work 
order backlog reduction; clearing a substantial portion, if not all, of 
aged mold and leak work orders at the selected sites; and uncovering 
systemic issues that can serve as lessons for the entire agency. The 
Monitors and NYCHA have identified the following lessons learned 
during the course of the MLRR program as areas NYCHA will look to 
improve on, for the program and agency-wide operations. 
First, the MLRR program has underscored the importance of 
addressing NYCHA’s challenges in accessing apartments. When 
NYCHA cannot get into an apartment, it cannot complete the work 
within the time it allots for the program, and it spends valuable 
program time sending staff to the same apartments repeatedly. 
NYCHA’s efforts here have been substantial. Across Whitman, 
Ingersoll, and Red Hook East, MLRR staff made between 84 and 91 
attempts per week to reach apartments with targeted work orders, 
including 
successful 
and 
unsuccessful 
attempts. 
Individual 
apartments sometimes receive a dozen attempts without staff ever 
gaining access. Critically, this is not an MLRR-specific problem, as 
access constrains repair work across NYCHA. The Monitors and 
NYCHA continue to evaluate NYCHA’s efforts to gain access, and the 
Monitors will work with NYCHA to ensure NYCHA’s efforts to gain 
access are well-tailored to reaching residents and that NYCHA is not 
inefficiently expending resources on residents that are unlikely to 
grant access. This includes initiatives in improving resident 
communications and NYCHA’s own efforts to hire a dedicated 
planner for the program.  
Second, a meaningful proportion of MLRR closures reflect 
verification by OCC that no repairs were needed, rather than 
completing physical work. This corresponds to the third column in the 
chart above. At Sotomayor, 43% of closed work orders involved no 
physical work, a figure that held roughly steady at Whitman (41.7%) 
before rising to 58.3% at Ingersoll and 58.9% at Red Hook East.153 
OCC verification played a significant role in proactively identifying 
these work orders: at Sotomayor, OCC verification accounted for 50% 
of work orders ultimately closed with no work done; at Whitman, 29%; 
and at Ingersoll and Red Hook East, 47% and 46%, respectively.

This verification work carries real value. By confirming with 
residents that certain repairs are no longer needed, OCC verification 
allows NYCHA to deploy resources more precisely and clears the 
path for more efficient future repairs. It also reflects the legacy of 
systemic record-keeping issues at NYCHA; while NYCHA has largely 
resolved those issues in recent years, a portion of the existing backlog 
stems from poor record-keeping in the past, and clearing those faulty 
entries requires meaningful effort. At the same time, this work 
advances the program’s primary purpose of resolving difficult, aged 
repair conditions only indirectly, and the Monitors note that the 
overall share of targeted work orders completed with physical repairs 
has not been rising. It bears noting that the balance of administrative 
and physical closures is not entirely within NYCHA’s direct control—
some work orders were never closed after work was already 
completed, some repairs were addressed by residents themselves, 
and some residents declined the work altogether because it was no 
longer needed. Nonetheless, the MLRR program’s longer-term 
impact on repair throughput is encouraging: the IDA found that the 
average number of mold and leak work orders resolved with work 
done since the end of MLRR at Whitman (70 work orders) was 63% 
higher than the pre-MLRR average (43 work orders). The Monitors 
and NYCHA share an interest in making full use of MLRR’s dedicated 
staff to perform the hands-on repair work the program was created to 
deliver and will continue tracking the balance of administrative and 
physical closures toward that end.  
NYCHA and the Monitors continue to regard MLRR as a 
substantial undertaking with significant potential to improve 
residents’ quality of life and to strengthen NYCHA’s repair and 
resident-communication capacity, a potential which NYCHA has 
already significantly realized. The Monitors will continue to identify 
measures to improve the program and work with NYCHA to 
implement them.

As part of its Transformation Plan, NYCHA has committed to 47

comply with other obligations under the HUD Agreement. The

benefits and utility of these initiatives in order to continually assess

The Monitors have identified two structural opportunities to 
help better assess the effectiveness of the Transformation Plan. First, 
the Transformation Project Management Office (“TPMO”) has built 
effective coordination and reporting functions, and there is an 
opportunity to build on that foundation by centralizing management 
of Transformation Plan initiatives and resource allocation across 
projects—enabling leadership to prioritize initiatives by impact and 
reallocate resources as needed. Second, and relatedly, the quality 
and availability of Key Performance Indicators (“KPIs”) varies across 
departments, which provides an opportunity to identify best practices

KPIs at the agency level would significantly strengthen NYCHA’s 
ability to manage and demonstrate impact. 
To that end, the Monitors look forward to working with NYCHA 
on the design and implementation of a Benefits Monitoring and 
Realization Framework. This framework will align existing projects 
with current KPIs, support the development of new KPIs, and establish 
a consistent methodology for assessing impact across departments. 
This will include compiling an inventory of existing KPIs and an 
assessment of how departments currently use them, followed by the 
collaborative development of a target vision that builds on efforts 
NYCHA has already undertaken in this space.

Apply: Under the definition agreed to by the

Baez v. NYCHA, No. 13 Civ. 8915 (“Baez”):  
Baez is a federal class action lawsuit brought by 
NYCHA residents suffering from asthma and 
living in apartments with mold and excessive 
moisture. NYCHA settled Baez in 2014. The 
resulting consent decrees impose various 
requirements on NYCHA that are similar but not 
identical to the HUD Agreement obligations and 
aim to help NYCHA effectively remediate mold 
and moisture in a timely manner. (Mold & Leaks) 
City Capital Action Plan (“CCAP”): Under the 
HUD Agreement, New York City must provide 
$2.2 billion in capital funding to NYCHA over a 
ten-year period starting in 2019 ($250 million per 
year for the first four years, and $200 million per 
year for the final six years). These funds are 
committed to capital projects and can only be 
spent pursuant to an Action Plan. The City 
Capital Action Plan was approved by the First 
Monitor on May 8, 2021, and was amended in 
September 2023. (Pests & Waste)

Integrated Pest Management (“IPM”): An 
environmentally 
friendly, 
commonsense 
approach to pest control. Unlike traditional pest 
management, 
which 
involves 
the 
routine

prevention of pests and uses pesticide only as 
needed. It involves multiple forms of pest 
controls, and has a four-tier approach: (1) 
identify pests and monitor progress; (2) set 
action thresholds; (3) prevent pests; and (4) 
control pests. (Pests & Waste)

Low Voltage Conditions: Outages that result 
from low voltage conditions. Low voltage 
conditions refer to when an elevator is not 
receiving enough voltage to continue operating.

Many elevators (including NYCHA’s) cannot 
operate at lower voltages. Elevators need a 
steady supply of electricity to run safely. When 
the electrical voltage drops too low, the 
elevator’s motor does not have enough power to 
move the elevator car, and the safety systems 
automatically shut the elevator down to prevent 
damage or unsafe operation. Low voltage 
conditions can occur from widespread (e.g., a 
grid goes down) or local (e.g., a transformer 
blows) electrical issues. Low voltage conditions 
generally require an electrician to resolve the 
issue at the source of the problem. In responding 
to all outages, NYCHA mechanics will take 
voltage readings to confirm if the outage is due 
to low voltage conditions. (Elevators) 
Mandatory Minimum Temperature: From 
October 1 to May 31 (the “Heating Season”), 
during the day (6:00 a.m. to 10:00 p.m.), 
apartments must be at least 68°F when the 
outside temperature is below 55°F, and at least 
62°F at night (10:00 p.m. to 6:00 a.m.) regardless 
of the outside temperature. (Heat) 
Modernization: Modernizing elevators refers to 
replacing key equipment and electronics and 
refurbishing the elevator cabs. (Elevators)

and is used to measure improvements across 
developments. (Organizational Change)

No-Service Condition: The HUD Agreement 
defines “no-service conditions” to include only a 
situation where all elevators are out of service at 
one building. However, NYCHA, HUD, and SDNY 
have agreed that it is consistent with the purpose 
of the Agreement for “no-service conditions” to 
also include a situation where all elevators that 
serve an apartment are out of service, which 
generally means all elevators in one bank are out 
of service. For example, a single building may 
have one bank of elevators that serves the 
northern half of the building, and a second bank 
of elevators that serves the southern half of the 
building. If all of the elevators that serve the 
northern half of the building stop working, that 
would be considered a no-service condition 
because the apartments on the northern half of 
the building cannot be accessed by elevator, 
even though other elevators in the building 
continue to function. (Elevators)

Pests: Under the HUD Agreement, the term

Remote

Monitoring 
Systems 
(“REMS”): REMS provide continuous real-time 
monitoring by tracking elevator performance 
through sensors that detect faults and generate 
fault codes. The local elevator motor room 
receives the fault codes in a fault log, then 
transmits the codes to either physical servers at 
the central office of NYCHA’s Elevator Service 
and Repair Department (“ESRD”) for older REMS 
devices (“server-based”), or to a cloud-based 
monitoring system for newer REMS devices 
(“cloud-based”), where the data can be accessed 
and used to coordinate responses, generate 
work orders, and monitor elevator performance 
offsite. (Elevators)
