March 10, 2025
Summary

While Mayor Eric Adams’ Fiscal Year 2026 Preliminary Budget is balanced on paper, it is short $3.6 billion needed to fund existing services and there is no plan to shrink large outyear budget gaps. The simple truth is by continuingly expanding and adding programs that are unaffordable in the long run, the City’s budget has become precarious, threatening to unravel over the next few years and putting the City in a poor position to respond to potentially significant federal aid cuts. The City should be making the needed hard choices now, so it is able to preserve critical services in the future. 

First, since fiscal year 2023, the City increasingly is spending more money than it is receiving each year. Adjusted for the City’s ability to shift the timing of paying some bills, expenses exceeded revenues by $636.0 million in fiscal year 2023, $1.1 billion in fiscal year 2024, and the imbalance may be even higher in fiscal year 2025. Still, the City has been able to balance its budget by using the surplus funds it had accumulated through fiscal year 2022. 

Second, these rolled surplus funds are quickly being depleted. They reached a high of $6.1 billion in fiscal year 2022 and will likely be closer to $3.0 billion at the end of fiscal year 2025. Soon, dwindling surplus funds from prior years may be insufficient to cover future shortfalls.

Third, the City’s habit of underbudgeting current services has grown substantially, exceeding $4.0 billion a year. This falsely gives the impression the City can afford to increase spending even more during budget negotiations, and it understates outyear budget gaps by billions of dollars.

Fourth, this precarious fiscal outlook leaves the City unprepared to respond to potentially significant reductions of federal aid and/or a recession.

Fifth, rapid spending growth for the CityFHEPS rental voucher program, NYC Health + Hospitals City subsidy, and special education “Carter Cases,” among other programs, will further weaken the City’s finances. Without policy and management changes, these are likely to persist and further widen budget gaps that will necessitate budget reductions. 

CBC found that:

Expenses exceeded revenues by $636.0 million in fiscal year 2023 and $1.1 billion in fiscal year 2024, adjusted for the City’s ability to shift timing of payments;
Year-end prepayment of expenses—which effectively rolls previously accumulated excess money from one year to the next—declined from $6.1 billion in fiscal year 2022 to $4.4 billion in fiscal year 2024, and is likely to decline further in fiscal year 2025; 
The fiscal year 2026 preliminary budget is short $3.6 billion needed to provide planned services;
This shortfall is comprised of underbudgeting $4.0 billion needed to maintain current service levels and $450.0 million to implement the State’s Class Size Reduction mandate, offset by $575.0 million in higher than budgeted State support and $266.0 million in overbudgeting for City-funded asylum seeker services; 
The City has already added $3.8 billion during fiscal year 2025 to fund programs that CBC identified as underbudgeted at the beginning of the fiscal year, and more will likely be needed;
During fiscal year 2024, the City added $4.0 billion to fund underbudgeted programs that year; and
Future budget gaps are $7.8 billion, $10.1 billion, and $9.6 billion in fiscal years 2027 to 2029, respectively, when underbudgeting, the Class Size Reduction mandate, and projected asylum seeker costs are taken into account. 

While the City’s short-term choices have increasingly stressed its finances, federal budget and tax policy changes may well reduce funds that support the City and State budgets. While there should be strong advocacy to push to maintain federal aid, both the City and the State will likely have to make hard choices about budget priorities. 

Unfortunately, the City’s precarious fiscal condition makes responding to federal cuts very difficult. As it has for decades, the City will balance the budget as required by law, but that balance is increasingly likely to be at the expense of critical services because those are often the easiest spending cuts to make when time is short. The City should take steps now to shore up its fiscal house. 

CBC recommends that the City:

End the budget gimmickry and present accurate, reasonable estimates of expenses and revenue, as required by the City Charter, in the Fiscal Year 2026 Executive Budget;
Implement a Program to Eliminate the Gap (PEG) to reduce spending growth by streamlining administrative operations, increasing program efficiency, and scaling back or eliminating programs that deliver the least value to residents; 
Not fund any new or expanded programs in the Executive Budget or during adoption; and
Add at least $1.0 billion in funding to the fiscal year 2026 General Reserve to help respond to potential federal cuts.  
City’s Surplus Roll Masks Mismatch Between Recurring Expenses and Revenues

Plainly put, New York City’s recurring expenses exceed its recurring revenues. The City prepays expenses for the upcoming year to roll resources across fiscal years—like paying January rent in December. This decades-long standard City practice has been a reasonable way to smooth out spending over the business cycle, since, until recently, the City had no rainy day fund. Typically, the prepayment grows during an economic expansion and shrinks during a recession as it gets “spent down.” By fiscal year 2022, the prepayment of accumulated surplus funds had grown to $6.1 billion. (See Figure 1.)

Concerningly, the prepayment has decreased substantially during the last two years: by $636.0 million and $1.1 billion, respectively. This means an increasing amount of the previously built-up surplus was needed to cover the gap between each year’s revenues and expenses. This was not a typical draw down during a recession but rather the City was using the funds to support unaffordable spending during a period of economic growth. 

Current-year spending remains underbudgeted by more than $700.0 million and, according to the City’s fiscal monitors, tax revenues may be roughly $335.0 million higher than forecast.[1] This suggests that the prepayment of fiscal year 2026 expenses, now projected to be $2.3 billion, is unlikely to increase much, let alone come close to last year’s $4.4 billion. This would continue the trend of spending exceeding revenue for a third year. This draw down of prior year funds to balance fiscal year 2025 will further deplete the amount available to address shortfalls next year and in the future.

The Preliminary Budget Undercounts Likely Annual Spending by $3.5 Billion to $4.7 Billion in Fiscal Years 2026 to 2028

Accounting for underbudgeting, overbudgeting for asylum seeker services, and State aid, the budget is roughly $430.0 million short this year and $3.6 billion short next year. (See Table 1.) Fiscal year 2027 is $3.5 billion short. Fiscal years 2028 and 2029 are even higher, $4.7 billion and $4.6 billion, because potential asylum seeker savings are much smaller.   

Underbudgeting Remains at Record Levels, Reaching $4 Billion a Year

Fiscal years 2026 to 2029 are underbudgeted by at least $4.0 billion a year for programs including CityFHEPS housing vouchers ($515.0 million), public assistance ($467.0 million), MTA subsidies ($268.0 million), Carter Cases ($131.0 million), and overtime ($682.0 million), to name a few.[2] Underbudgeting is no secret and the fiscal monitors agree that the City budget hides billions in expenses.[3]

This practice impedes transparency and compromises the public’s trust in the budget process. It distorts the financial plan that is the basis for budget negotiations and decisions. It encourages the City’s leaders to spend more money than the City can support in the long run and defers the challenge of identifying resources until after the budget is adopted.

Although it is common for some planned costs to increase after the budget is adopted, the extent of underbudgeting has increased radically in recent years. Between fiscal years 2015 and 2021, the City added an average of $783.0 million a year after the budget was adopted to maintain service levels. (See Figure 2.) This doubled to $1.5 billion in fiscal years 2022 and 2023. Then, during fiscal year 2024, the City added $4.1 billion for expenses, excluding migrant services. Just halfway through fiscal year 2025, the City had already added $3.8 billion for programs that were underbudgeted at adoption and fiscal year 2025 remains underbudgeted by $718.0 million, by CBC’s estimate.[4]

City Budget Does Not Include Cost of Complying with State Class Size Mandate

Complying with the State’s Class Size Reduction mandate will cost money, mainly to hire more teachers, beginning in fiscal year 2026.[5] (The City was already in compliance for the fiscal years 2024 and 2025, so costs were minimal.) However, compliance is not reflected in the budget and is estimated to cost $450.0 million next year, $900.0 million in fiscal year 2027, and $1.4 billion a year in fiscal year 2028 and beyond.[6] The City could choose to shift funds from other programs within or outside education or identify savings, but it has yet to support this strategy, which would require programmatic specifics and have budget implications.

The Cost of Asylum Seeker Services is Overstated

The Preliminary Budget forecasts asylum seeker services will cost $3.3 billion in fiscal year 2025 and $2.7 billion in fiscal year 2026. However, if the census continues its current trajectory, CBC estimates that the City will need to spend $2.9 billion this year and $1.4 billion next year.[7] 

However, only a portion of these savings would reduce the City’s planned spending. The City budget assumes $1.0 billion of State aid next year that is not included in the State’s Fiscal Year 2026 Budget.[8] Therefore, just $266.0 million of the $1.3 billion in expected asylum seeker savings over two years would be available to spend on other programs; the rest would offset non-existing State aid. 

New York State Fiscal Year 2026 Executive Budget Provides City with Nearly $600 Million Not Included in the City Budget 

On net, the State budget will increase City funding by $595.0 million, 3.1 percent of fiscal year 2026 budgeted State funds. Governor Kathy Hochul’s Fiscal Year 2026 Executive Budget includes proposals that both increase and decrease State support for the City. (See Table 2.) 

The biggest increase would be in State education aid, which is proposed to be $703.0 million higher than State fiscal year 2025.[9] However, the City would have received an additional $350 million had the State not proposed Foundation Aid formula changes that reduced City funding compared to the existing formula.[10] Nonetheless, as the City budget had assumed almost no growth in State aid, the full $703.0 million will be an increase in the City’s budget.[11]

Other State resources being provided to the City include $58.0 million for NYPD subway deployments (which the City has agreed to match), $36.0 million for assigned counsel, $14.0 million for enhanced subway outreach, $12.0 million for youth services, and $16.0 million in other increases.[12] These resources are offset by proposals that would drive up City spending: a mandated $165.0 million increase in the City’s MTA paratransit contribution; discontinuation of $57.0 million in Public Hospital Indigent Care Pool Payments; and changes to Early Intervention that will increase City costs by $20 million. 

Outyear Budget Gaps Reach $8 Billion to $10 Billion

Adding the identified spending adjustments demonstrates that fiscal year 2026 is $3.6 billion short for current service levels, and the future budget gaps increase to $7.8 billion, $10.1 billion, and $9.6 billion in fiscal years 2027 to 2029, respectively. (See Figure 3.)

Upward Spending Pressures Could Further Widen Gaps

The Preliminary Budget proposed to increase City funds spending by 2 percent in fiscal year 2026, but this is not the whole story. If past is prologue, the Executive and Adopted Budget will increase spending even more. Between fiscal years 2022 and 2024, an average of $3.3 billion of new City-funded spending was added to the executive and adopted budgets, plus another $500 million for City Council initiatives.[13] These large additions drive up future budget gaps.

Furthermore, program costs typically increase with inflation and collective bargaining agreements, sometimes more than budgeted. Some programs, however, have grown at far faster rates in recent years, most notably the CityFHEPs housing voucher program, Carter Cases, and the City subsidy to Health + Hospitals.[14] Absent a plan to rein in spending growth for these programs, outyear gaps will widen further. 

City Reserves are Insufficient

The City currently holds only $2.0 billion in the Revenue Stabilization Funds (RSF, also referred to as the Rainy Day Fund), which is insufficient as the average recession would sap $11.0 billion in revenue over two years.[15] The City has not made a deposit to the RSF since fiscal year 2022, despite strong tax revenue growth of 4.6 percent. Rainy Days Funds should be used only to respond to a short-term event, like a recession or other temporary emergency.

The City also has $5 billion in the Retiree Health Benefits Trust (RHBT) that has been set aside for other than postemployment benefit (OPEB) liabilities but has been used as a de facto reserve in the past.[16] While it may be tempting to use these funds during fiscal distress, as with the RSF, that would be applying a one-time band-aid to a recurring challenge, while sapping funds that should be held for their intended purpose—to support future health benefits for retirees. 

The City’s budget also includes $1.5 billion in budgetary reserves per year beginning in fiscal year 2026. These funds are spent down during the year and can be used to offset unexpected increases; however, the amount is below projected budget gaps.

Conclusion
NYC Not Fiscally Prepared For Federal Cuts

The stark reality of the City’s budget—unaffordable rapid spending growth, current year expenditures that exceed current year revenues, unrealistically low spending projections, and large future gaps—is that New York City finances will weaken and become a serious risk during the next few years, even absent a recession.

While this is precarious enough, even more concerning is that the City is not prepared to handle the fiscal impact of likely federal budget cuts. Predicting specifics of the magnitude, timing, and affected programs is extremely difficult. But the City should prepare, both fiscally and strategically.

The City will not be able to fill all the holes created by federal funding reductions. If there are significant federal cuts, the City should use its own resources as a bridge to a likely new lower federal aid funding level and focus programs on those that are most impactful for the most significant New York needs.

 In order to shore up its fiscal foundation, CBC recommends that the City:

End the budget gimmickry and present accurate, reasonable estimates of expenses and revenue, as required by the City Charter, in the Fiscal Year 2026 Executive Budget;
Implement a Program to Eliminate the Gap (PEG) to reduce spending growth by streamlining administrative operations, increasing program efficiency, and scaling back or eliminating programs that deliver the least value to residents; 
Not fund any additional new or expanded programs in the Executive Budget or during adoption; and

Add at least $1.0 billion in funding to the fiscal year 2026 General Reserve to help respond to potential federal cuts.
