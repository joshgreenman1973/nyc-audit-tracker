EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
On January 31, 2019, the New York City Housing Authority 
(“NYCHA”) committed to implementing essential reforms to improve 
housing conditions for NYCHA residents through an agreement (the 
“HUD Agreement” or “Agreement”) with the U.S. Attorney’s Ofﬁce for 
the Southern District of New York (“SDNY”), the U.S. Department of 
Housing and Urban Development (“HUD”), and the City of New York 
(the “City”). As part of the terms, a monitor was appointed to oversee 
and evaluate adherence to the Agreement. After the First Monitor 
completed a ﬁve-year term, on February 28, 2024, the current co-
Monitors (“Monitors”), Neil Barofsky and Matthew Cipolla of Jenner & 
Block LLP (“Jenner”), began their ﬁve-year term. This is their ﬁfth 
report. 
In their prior reports, available on the Monitors’ website, the 
Monitors detailed their assessment of NYCHA’s compliance with the 
HUD Agreement, including NYCHA’s successes and challenges. In 
this report, and others going forward, the Monitors continue to 
provide regular updates on NYCHA’s progress in meeting those 
requirements. This report includes data from May 1, 2025, to July 31, 
2025.  
NYCHA has continued to build on the progress discussed in 
the last report, for which it should be commended, with important 
areas for improvement detailed below and in the body of the report.  
The Monitors again recognize and thank those who are 
working to improve NYCHA and help it fully meet its obligations 
under the HUD Agreement, including the constructive and 
collaborative approach taken by NYCHA’s leadership and staff; the 
vital feedback received from the NYCHA residents and resident 
leaders who have taken time to engage with the Monitors; and the 
expertise and engagement from SDNY and HUD officials and other 
stakeholders. 
Property Management 
NYCHA committed in the HUD Agreement to various 
obligations related to heat, elevators, pests and waste, lead, mold, 
and inspections. Further detail concerning these topics is contained 
in Section I of this report. As described further in that section, NYCHA 
has achieved significant milestones, including:

I.1 
Heat 
The HUD Agreement requires NYCHA to improve heating 
conditions for NYCHA residents through a variety of measures, such 
as (1) replacing a specified number of boilers, (2) establishing 
systems for responding to apartments affected by heating failures, (3) 
investigating failures to timely restore heat, (4) notifying residents of 
heating outages, (5) monitoring the temperature in apartments, and 
(6) reducing the duration and number of heating failures experienced 
by residents.1 
For the first time, this report presents data regarding heating 
failures caused both by central heating system components, like 
boilers, and in-apartment components, like radiators, as well as the 
amount of time it takes NYCHA to restore heat to individual 
apartments in response to verified heating complaints. This is a 
significant development because, although certain HUD Agreement 
obligations have been in effect since the inception of the monitorship, 
until the work completed now by NYCHA and the current Monitors, 
there was no agreed-upon methodology to measure NYCHA’s 
performance with respect to individual in-apartment heating failures. 
With this methodology in place, the Monitors can now provide a more 
complete picture of how heating failures affect residents and how 
quickly NYCHA restores service.  
Different terminology is used to refer to these different types 
of heating system component failures. Heating outages refer to 
failures of central heating system components, such as boilers or 
risers, that prevent the system from delivering heat to multiple 
apartments. In-apartment heating failures refer to failures of 
heating system components located in residents’ apartments, such as 
radiators or radiator valves. 2  Both types of heating failures are 
collectively referred to as heating service interruptions. 
This report serves as the Monitors’ annual heat report, covering 
NYCHA’s full performance during the 2024‒2025 heating season, 
which ended May 31, 2025. Analyzing data on certain obligations for 
the first time revealed the extent to which NYCHA residents 
experience heating service interruptions. Specifically, in the 2024‒
2025 season, 84% of occupied apartments experienced at least one 
heating service interruption, the highest level since the monitorship 
began. Importantly, this does not mean that 84% of occupied 
apartments had temperatures below the legally required minimum;

Overall heating demand, as measured by HDDs, increased by 
approximately 4%, but the demand in the peak winter months—
December, January, and February—was approximately 17% greater

According to NYCHA, these colder conditions led to fewer planned

season because NYCHA generally does not conduct planned

corrective maintenance and repairs, contributed to the increase in 
unplanned outages during the 2024‒2025 heating season, because 
fewer opportunities to conduct such maintenance and repairs results 
in the failure of heating components. NYCHA also explained that

C. 
Mandatory Minimum Indoor Temperature Violations and 
Accompanying Action Plan (Requirement Nos. H6-H8) 
The HUD Agreement requires NYCHA to create a plan to track 
apartment temperatures and ensure apartments stay above the 
mandatory minimum temperatures required by the New York City 
Code during the heating season.12 Beginning with the 2024‒2025 
heating season, the Agreement sets limits on instances where an 
apartment’s temperature falls below the mandatory minimum indoor 
temperature.13 The Agreement specifies that NYCHA’s compliance 
with these obligations will be assessed according to procedures 
agreed upon in an Action Plan.14 
This report presents, for the first time, contextual information 
relevant to NYCHA’s performance on these HUD Agreement 
obligations. As discussed below, because no reliable in-apartment 
temperature data are available, the Monitors and NYCHA have 
developed an interim methodology to track the number of occupied 
apartments impacted by heating outages and in-apartment heating 
failures. The results show that both the share of apartments 
experiencing at least one heating outage or in-apartment heating 
failure and those experiencing four or more such issues increased 
compared to the prior heating season.  
As noted above, NYCHA has attributed this increase to several 
factors. It points first to the colder winter conditions, which limited 
opportunities for planned outages that, in turn, contributed to more 
unplanned outages. NYCHA also cites the continued challenges of 
aging infrastructure, noting that boilers, control systems, and 
distribution systems are often well past their useful life and more 
prone to breakdowns. In addition, as the Monitors have previously 
reported, NYCHA states that following the April 2024 termination of 
third-party heating vendors, it assumed responsibility for more than 
4,000 pieces of heating and hot water equipment without a 
corresponding increase in staffing levels. NYCHA notes that this 
mismatch between equipment and staffing also contributed to the 
rise in outages during the first full heating season following the 
transition, as it adapted to the added responsibility.

D. 
Heat Restoration Obligations (Requirement Nos. H6, H9, 
H11) 
The HUD Agreement requires NYCHA to restore heat in a 
timely manner when an apartment loses heat. Since its inception, the 
Agreement has required NYCHA to restore heat to affected 
apartments, on average, within 12 hours per heating service 
interruption.22 Beginning with the 2024‒2025 heating season, the 
Agreement requires NYCHA to restore heat to affected apartments 
within 12 hours 85% of the time, and to restore heat to affected 
apartments within 24 hours for 100% of the time.23  
This report marks the first time the Monitors are presenting 
NYCHA’s compliance with the HUD Agreement obligations for 
restoration times following in-apartment heating failures, as indicated 
by residents’ verified heat complaints. During the 2024‒2025 heating 
season, NYCHA met all heat restoration obligations for heating 
outages. While it did not meet these obligations for in-apartment 
heating failures, NYCHA nevertheless showed marked year-over-year 
improvement in the last two heating seasons, as detailed in the 
sections below.  
NYCHA attributes the long gap in returning to performance 
levels last seen in the 2019‒2020 heating season to a combination of 
factors. After the HUD Agreement was signed in January 2019, 
NYCHA reports that it principally focused on restoring heat as quickly 

28 
 
28 
SECTION I: PROPERTY MANAGEMENT  
as possible to address heating outages that affected multiple 
apartments. This effort, while improving NYCHA’s heating outage 
response, contributed to slower responses to verified complaints 
regarding in-apartment heating failures. According to NYCHA, the 
problem was compounded during the COVID-19 period, when it 
relied heavily on third-party vendors to manage sites. Performance 
began to decline after the 2019‒2020 heating season and worsened 
steadily, reaching its lowest point in the 2022‒2023 season, when 
NYCHA’s Heating Management Services Department (“HMSD”) says 
it also faced high vacancy levels. Vendor-managed sites added 
further complications—because, according to NYCHA, vendors 
lacked access to NYCHA’s systems, and property management staff 
had to close out complaints on their behalf after the fact, often doing 
so late as they processed numerous complaints in addition to 
heating. These factors slowed response times. Since then, NYCHA 
notes that it has sought to correct these problems by reclaiming 
vendor-managed sites, rebuilding its heating staffing pipeline, and 
improving data integrity. 
NYCHA specifically credits its more recent improvement to 
operational changes that emphasize data-driven decision-making 
and increased accountability. NYCHA increased its focus on key 
performance indicators to identify potential emergencies before they 
materialized. The agency also noted that it began reviewing work 
orders, resident complaint backlog, and infrastructure deficiencies to 
efficiently deploy its staff and resources, and it instituted daily check-
ins with frontline staff to ensure real-time accountability. In addition, 
NYCHA held regular meetings to discuss the worst performers to 
guide its maintenance and investment strategies. NYCHA believes 
that these efforts account for the progress it has made over the past 
two heating seasons.

Although a NYCHA elevator can usually continue to operate 
with a 5% voltage reduction, if the voltage is reduced by more than 
5%, most NYCHA elevators will automatically shut down to prevent 
damage or unsafe conditions. 44  On certain dates during this 
reporting period, Con Edison reduced voltage by greater than 5%, 
which caused multiple unplanned outages in NYCHA’s elevators. 
Outages caused by voltage reductions can leave passengers stuck in 
elevators, leave residents without elevator service, damage elevator 
equipment, and strain the resources of NYCHA’s Elevator Service and 
Repair Department (ESRD). Of course, blackouts, which are avoided 
by Con Edison’s voltage reductions, would likely cause far more 
severe consequences for residents. 
Con Edison initiates voltage reductions to protect its grid and 
contacts New York City’s Emergency Management (“NYCEM”) to 
notify NYCEM of the extent of the reductions and the areas that will 
be impacted. NYCHA is included in these communications, and when 
a reduction is confirmed, NYCHA’s Emergency Management and 
Systems Department (“EMSD”) alerts the rest of NYCHA, including 
ESRD, about when and where the voltage reductions will take place. 
It can be as quick as a few minutes between the time a determination 
to reduce voltage is made and the time it is implemented, meaning 
NYCHA may have little time to adjust its operations. 
Once ESRD receives the Con Edison notifications, mechanics 
will take affected elevators offline or confirm that the elevators were 
automatically shut down due to low voltage conditions. In both cases, 
NYCHA’s mechanics will check to see if anyone is in the elevator and 
help safely remove any person who may be stuck. They then place 
written postings at each elevator opening to alert residents. When 
voltage is restored, elevators do not come back online automatically. 
Rather, ESRD mechanics must check each elevator before bringing it 
back online. Because automatic shutdowns from voltage reduction 
events often cause minor component failures, such as blown fuses, 
ESRD mechanics must repair each affected elevator, which creates a 
delay between restoration of normal voltage and resumption of 
elevator service.

Voltage reductions this summer caused numerous elevator 
outages. From June 1, 2025, to July 31, 2025, NYCHA elevators 
experienced 682 low voltage outages with an average duration of 
almost 29 hours per outage. By way of comparison, during the same 
period in 2024, NYCHA elevators experienced 193 low voltage 
outages with an average duration of approximately 19 hours per 
outage. 
A small number of NYCHA’s elevators are able to operate 
safely even when voltage reductions exceed 5% because NYCHA has 
installed Automatic Voltage Regulators (“AVRs”).45 AVRs are devices 
installed on existing elevators that stabilize incoming electrical 
voltage, allowing elevators to continue operating safely and reliably 
despite fluctuations in power supply. NYCHA has installed AVRs on 
13 elevators, 12 of which were installed before last summer. Across 
those 12 elevators, low voltage outages declined from 29 between 
2019 and 2023, to just 2 in 2024 and 2025. The best example is at 
Roosevelt Houses, where one elevator experienced 14 low voltage 
outages between January 1, 2019, and August 2, 2023, but only a 
single outage after an AVR was installed on August 2, 2023. Based on 
available cost estimates, installing an AVR for an elevator costs 
between $45,000 and $60,000, including equipment and labor. With 
NYCHA operating 2,938 passenger elevators as of July 31, 2025, it 
would cost over $100 million to install AVRs across all NYCHA 
developments. 
As NYCHA replaces or modernizes elevators, it equips them 
with Uninterrupted Power Supply (“UPS”) systems, which are required 
by the New York City Building Code. While AVRs allow elevators to 
continue operating during voltage fluctuations, NYCHA’s UPS 
systems serve a limited safety function. They provide emergency 
power during voltage reductions or a blackout, giving the elevator 
enough power to reach the next floor and allow passengers to exit. 
Unlike AVRs, which help prevent low voltage outages from occurring 
in the first place, UPS systems ensure safe passenger egress when 
service is interrupted. 
Many of NYCHA’s older elevators will not be replaced for years 
and will not have either AVR or UPS systems. NYCHA has secured 
funding to install a few more AVRs and the Monitors are supporting 
NYCHA in identifying locations that would be most benefited by 
installation. Additional funding for the installation of AVRs on existing

The HUD Agreement requires NYCHA to timely remediate 
mold and leaks, prevent mold growth and recurrence, and stop 
NYCHA employees from improperly closing mold-related work

separate set of obligations established by court orders in a federal 
class action lawsuit, Baez v. NYCHA, No. 13 Civ. 8915 (“Baez”), 
brought by NYCHA residents to address mold and leaks in NYCHA 
developments.101  
The subsections below provide an update on NYCHA’s 
compliance with the outstanding mold and leak obligations under the 
HUD Agreement as of July 31, 2025, as well as information about 
mold-related capital projects. 
A. 
Timely Remediation of Mold and Leaks (Requirements Nos. 
M5-M7) 
The HUD Agreement requires NYCHA to timely address 
residents’ mold and leak complaints. Specifically, NYCHA must timely 
(1) remove or remediate mold and its root cause(s) within specified 
timeframes, (2) provide residents with work plans that explain how 
NYCHA will address mold and its root cause(s), and (3) abate the root 
cause(s) of sustained moisture and remove all resulting standing 
water (collectively, the “Response-Time Obligations”).102  
1. 
Removal and Remediation of Mold (Obligation No. 
M6) 
The HUD Agreement requires NYCHA to remove or 
remediate mold and its root cause(s) within specified timeframes at 
least 95% of the time.103  
The timeframes NYCHA must meet when addressing residents’ 
mold complaints depend on the complexity of the repair work. Under 
the HUD Agreement, NYCHA must remove mold within five business 
days of a resident’s mold complaint or remediate mold and its root 
cause(s) within seven days for a simple repair or 15 days for a 
complex repair.104  
The rate at which NYCHA removes or remediates mold and its 
root cause(s) within these timeframes is known as the “blended rate.”

limited to, mold and leak work orders) in the BLI apartments,

In furtherance of its commitment to prioritize childcare centers, 
in the first half of Year 7 NYCHA reviewed its property portfolio to

this review, NYCHA conducted a field study of 190 community 
centers on its properties to determine which ones were being used

federal, state, and local lead regulations.  
NYCHA plans to take several steps towards addressing these

plans to conduct comprehensive XRF testing of all of the centers

identifies deficiencies or deteriorated paint, it will promptly conduct 
XRF testing of those components and perform interim controls where 
necessary.

as soon as possible following the completion of XRF testing in 2026.

Repairs of Deficiencies (Requirement No. I4) 
Under the HUD Agreement, NYCHA is required to complete 
minor repairs of deficiencies discovered during annual apartment 
inspections. As part of its process, NYCHA maintenance workers 
identify if a repair is required. If it is, it is either completed at the time 
of inspection or deferred. A repair may be deferred if, for example, 
the repair would be too time-consuming, or the maintenance worker 
does not have the proper equipment available. A repair that is 
deferred will be scheduled for repair at a later date.

5,126 deficiencies in 3,403 apartments. As described in the Monitors’ 
previous reports, NYCHA is working to improve its documentation 
and oversight of minor repairs.

Under the HUD Agreement, NYCHA is required to create an

Under the HUD Agreement, NYCHA is required to make broad 
management and organizational reforms across the agency that will 
improve its overall functioning and its delivery of services to 
residents. As discussed in the Monitors’ prior reports, NYCHA’s plan 
for doing so is contained in its “Transformation Plan,” which lays out 
broad principles for improvement and core values for the 
organization—e.g., “creating a culture of service,” “strengthening 
NYCHA’s partnership with its residents,” and “using data to drive 
decision making”—as well as 47 specific initiatives intended to 
improve NYCHA’s processes and performance. 164 Consistent with 
those broad principles, NYCHA also works with HUD, SDNY, and the 
Monitors to identify additional initiatives that will build on those 
specific projects to continue to improve itself as an organization.

developments over the last quarter within key areas relating to 
NYCHA’s organizational change.

1. 
Missed Repair Appointments 
Residents raised concerns to the Monitors that skilled trades 
workers often do not show up for scheduled appointments. The 
Monitors have been supporting NYCHA to reduce the frequency that 
this occurs.165  The Monitors developed a new Missed Appointments 
Dashboard for NYCHA, which NYCHA now uses as part of monthly

NYCHA is showing improvements in reducing the frequency 
that skilled trades appointments are missed. In January 2025, when 
the dashboard was first launched, NYCHA skilled trades staff were 
missing 7% of their scheduled repair appointments. But six months 
later, in July 2025, NYCHA skilled trades staff missed 4% of their 
scheduled repair appointments. Overall, for January to July 2025, 
NYCHA has missed 5% of its scheduled skilled trades repair 
appointments.

NYCHA currently lacks a unified, agency-wide approach to

As previously reported, the Monitor team developed a

time employees, it can conduct about 9,000 inspections

adopted a risk-based sampling approach, to ensure that

Ancillary Equipment: Ancillary equipment 
refers to building level systems that support the 
delivery of heat and hot water, such as vacuum 
tanks,

water lines, and heat control panels. (Heat) 
Application of Mold-Resistant Paint: To 
prevent recurrence, antimicrobial mold-resistant 
paint is applied to walls after mold is removed. 
(Mold & Leaks) 
Apply: Under the definition agreed to by the 
First Monitor and NYCHA, this is the time from 
the moment a complaint is verified to the 
moment that NYCHA staff completes the initial 
work order for inspection and initial treatment. 
Typically, the same NYCHA staff member who 
responds to the initial work order applies the 
initial pest control treatment during the same 
visit. Application of pest control methods should 
take, on average, 45 minutes. (Pests & Waste) 
Baez v. NYCHA, No. 13 Civ. 8915 (“Baez”):  
Baez is a federal class action lawsuit brought by 
NYCHA residents suffering from asthma and 
living in apartments with mold and excessive 
moisture. NYCHA settled Baez in 2014. The 
resulting consent decrees impose various 
requirements on NYCHA that are similar but not 
identical to the HUD Agreement obligations and 
aim to help NYCHA effectively remediate mold 
and moisture in a timely manner. (Mold & Leaks) 
Building 
Management 
Systems 
(“BMS”): 
Computerized controls that provide automation, 
remote monitoring, and remote control for

No-Service Condition: The HUD Agreement

situation where all elevators are out of service at 
one building. However, NYCHA, HUD, and SDNY 
have agreed that it is consistent with the purpose 
of the Agreement for “no-service conditions” to 
also include a situation where all elevators that 
serve an apartment are out of service, which 
generally means all elevators in one bank are out 
of service. For example, a single building may 
have one bank of elevators that serves the 
northern half of the building, and a second bank 
of elevators that serves the southern half of the 
building. If all elevators that serve the northern 
half of the building stop working, that would be 
considered a no-service condition because the 
apartments on the northern half of the building 
cannot be accessed by elevator, even though 
other elevators in the building continue to 
function. (Elevators)

Voltage Reductions: During heat waves, 
demand for electricity spikes as the use of 
cooling devices, such as fans and air 
conditioners, increases. If demand exceeds 
available capacity, equipment powering the 
electrical grid can overheat and fail, leading to 
blackouts. Reducing the voltage provided to the 
grid reduces overall electricity use, which lowers 
the risk of equipment failure or blackout. 
However, many elevators (including NYCHA’s) 
cannot operate at lower voltages. Elevators 
need a steady supply of electricity to run safely. 
When the electrical voltage drops too low, the 
elevator’s motor does not have enough power 
to move the elevator car, and the safety systems 
automatically shut the elevator down to prevent 
damage or unsafe operation. (Elevators)

future. Under the Mold Standard Procedure, a 
work plan is automatically generated following a 
verified mold inspection. (Mold & Leaks)
