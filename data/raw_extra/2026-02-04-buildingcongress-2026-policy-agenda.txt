BOLD, AMBITIOUS GOALS FOR 2026
The New York Building Congress is stepping into 2026 with the same roll-up-our-
sleeves optimism and energy that built our city’s iconic skyline. A new calendar 
year means fresh chances to partner with City Hall, Albany, and Washington to 
build bigger, break ground faster, cut red tape, put people to work, and keep New 
York’s economy moving. 
The building industry’s hard work, resilience, adaptability, and innovation continue 
to be a source of inspiration. Despite ongoing economic fluctuations and high 
interest rates, our members have not only persevered but excelled, showing once 
again that our industry is a major source of economic growth and prosperity in 
New York and the nation. 
The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (BIL) will expire in 2026. Our top priority 
is ensuring a new, continuous flow of federal infrastructure spending within 
a robust Surface Transportation Reauthorization bill. The BIL was an essential 
shot in the arm, and we as an organization will continue to fight for long-term, 
recurring commitments to fund vital projects and repairs, stewarded by the 
expertise of our members, to rebuild our aging infrastructure.  
We also look forward to working closely with the new Mayor on his 
administration’s priorities when it comes to building housing, shoring up our 
resiliency, and finding innovative infrastructure projects to ensure that New 
York remains the greatest city in the world. Additionally, working with our State 
partners will be important to ensure there are incentives to achieve all our housing 
and infrastructure goals.
Our objectives in 2026 are both bold and achievable. The Building Congress will 
remain the industry’s convening table and loudest voice, sharing data, shaping 
policy, and telling the story of a sector that fuels New York’s economy and 
provides pathways to the middle class for so many. 
Here’s to another year of building a stronger, smarter, more resilient New York.

INTRODUCTION
For more than a century, the New York Building Congress has rallied architects, engineers, 
contractors, subcontractors, labor, owners, and consultants behind one goal: building the things 
that keep people working and communities thriving. We are dedicated  to supporting initiatives that 
drive job creation and create a stronger economy for all New Yorkers. Our 2026 Policy Agenda 
pairs on-the-ground industry expertise with commonsense policy reforms that will spur growth, 
shorten approval timelines, and drive investment to where it is needed most.
The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (BIL) is 
unlikely to be reauthorized in its current form, 
but much of the spending package can be 
reconfigured into a Surface Transportation 
Reauthorization bill. We want to ensure that 
infrastructure funds from this new bill are 
adequate and allocated swiftly to projects across 
America, particularly in the New York region. It 
is imperative that a new, comprehensive funding 
bill is created to solidify the gains our nation has 
made in terms of infrastructure improvements 
and to ensure that our national infrastructure 
lasts for generations. 
Specifically, we call for:
1.	 Faster release of federal infrastructure 
funding and to ensure that New York 
gets its fair share of funding relative to its 
population, size of its public transit systems 
and the amount it contributes to the federal 
tax base and GDP.
2.	 Robust provisions to provide workers with 
skills needed to fill infrastructure jobs by 
creating new grants/funding for industry 
partnerships as well as special H-1B Visa 
exemptions for government-funded 
infrastructure projects.
3.	 Better tracking and transparency of 
infrastructure spending at the Federal level.
4.	 More streamlined and expedited 
environmental reviews as well as permitting 
reforms for infrastructure projects at all 
levels of government, and reforms to reduce 
the cost of construction.
5.	 Stabilize the Highway Trust Fund with 
predictable, reliable revenue sources so that 
states can better plan multi-year projects.
INFRASTRUCTURE FUNDING

1.	 Securing a strong federal infrastructure bill and long-term infrastructure investments, namely

Streamlining Environmental Reviews
To ensure that federal infrastructure funding 
is spent in the most effective and efficient 
manner, we support reforms to improve 
environmental reviews to avoid costly 
project delays. The Federal Department of 
Transportation issued major updates in July 
2025 that expand use of categorical exclusions 
(CEs), set page and schedule limits, and 
standardize required documentation. We seek 
to codify and enact lasting reforms to the 
National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) 
to further expedite reviews for eligible projects 
as well as expanding which projects qualify for 
CEs across all agencies.
We will also push for state-level expansions of 
Type 2 Actions and broader efforts to improve 
and streamline State Environmental Quality 
Review Act (SEQRA) policies. Governor 
Hochul’s “Let Them Build” agenda, with 
common sense SEQRA reforms, is a critically 
important initiative for this year’s legislative 
session.
Specifically, we support the following:
H.R. 4776, Standardizing Permitting and 
Expediting Economic Development  
(SPEED) Act
The bipartisan SPEED Act would modernize 
NEPA by narrowing and standardizing 
environmental review procedures. It aims to 
make reviews more predictable by clarifying 
what agencies must analyze and strengthens 
the lead agency’s role in defining scope 
and interagency coordination. Regarding 
litigation, it seeks to set clearer guardrails on 
how and when challenges can be made, to 
limit prolonged disputes that can stall critical 
infrastructure even after a substantial technical 
review has been completed.
S. 1449, Revitalizing the Economy by 
Simplifying Timelines and Assuring 
Regulatory Transparency (RESTART) Act
The RESTART Act would streamline agency 
reviews with enforceable timelines, add time 
limits to prevent endless legal challenges, and 
modernize existing laws. 
NY State: S3492 / A6283: Sustainable 
Affordable Housing and Sprawl Prevention 
Act (May/Kelles)
This bill would strengthen and streamline 
SEQRA by creating clear exemptions and 
guardrails for qualifying infill and transit-
oriented housing in already developed 
areas, keeping reviews focused on genuine 
environmental impacts while reducing abuse of 
the environmental review process to delay or 
block housing.

 BIG NEW IDEA FOR 2026
NATIONAL INFRASTRUCTURE RESERVE BOARD (NIRB):
The Federal Reserve was created to keep the national economy stable by 
placing monetary policy under an independent governing board, insulated from 
day-to-day politics. So, too, is it vital to protect our national infrastructure in 
a similar way. As such, we propose creating a “National Infrastructure Reserve 
Board,” an independent, nonpartisan institution modeled on the Federal 
Reserve. 
Its core mandate is simple: safeguard the nation’s state-of-good-repair and 
replacement needs so that critical infrastructure does not fall into disrepair 
from lack of timely investment, or political convenience. Establishing the 
NIRB in statute and providing a standing, mandatory, appropriation that 
operates outside of the annual appropriations cycle could make this a reality. 
The NIRB would focus on the reliability of bridges, tunnels, transit networks, 
water systems, energy networks, and other critical public works. Discretionary 
new megaprojects, however, would still move through regular congressional 
budget approval. 
Governance would mirror the Fed: a small national board of governors, 
appointed to staggered, lengthy terms, removable only for cause. Regional 
councils would provide local planning and engineering expertise. Their purview 
would cover multi-year capital planning and formula-based allocations. The 
board would publish an annual “State of Public Works” report and quarterly 
updates to ensure accountability and transparency. NIRB decisions would 
be grounded in measurable need, lifecycle cost analysis, risk, and resilience 
criteria.
By separating essential infrastructure projects from the politics of the 
budget cycle, NIRB would ultimately save taxpayer dollars by reducing 
costly emergency repairs, while ensuring that our national infrastructure, the 
foundation of our economic stability, remains intact.

A safe, affordable home is the foundation of 
every thriving community. Yet New York still 
builds far too few to keep pace with surging 
demand. People want to live here and start 
families here, but, too often, the cost of 
housing is a barrier to that dream. The most 
effective way to bring down housing costs long 
term for all New Yorkers is by significantly 
increasing our housing supply to meet sky-high 
demand. New York needs a housing strategy 
that matches the scale of our current crisis.
485-x is finally in place and we’re starting 
to see early projects line up, but not at the 
pace required to close the gap. City of Yes 
for Housing Opportunity was a strong step 
that can open the door to tens of thousands 
of homes over time, yet we still need more 
tools today to unlock production at the level 
we need. We need faster approvals, wider 
rezonings, deeper as-of-right allowability, and 
robust, long-term incentives that create more 
mixed-income housing developments. 
Increasing our housing supply is essential for 
maintaining a healthy fiscal environment, 
increasing state and local tax revenues, as well 
as more paid riders on transit and commuter 
rail lines.
Current estimates suggest that New York City 
may only see about 21,000 new units in 2026, 
still far from sufficient to close the existing gap, 
which numbers in the hundreds of thousands. 
Our housing crisis and associated lack of 
population growth is directly detrimental to our 
congressional standing and representation in 
Washington, D.C. To avoid losing House seats 
and to genuinely alleviate the housing crisis, we 
would need to build more than 800,000 new 
homes statewide by 2030.
Housing Supply Acceleration:
485-x: What’s Next?
New York’s 485-x program was a significant 
effort to fill the gap left by the expiration of 
the 421-a tax incentive, aiming to create a 
boom in residential development after a period 
of slow growth. However, there are growing 
concerns that 485-x may not be structured to 
meaningfully support the level of homebuilding 
activity needed in New York.
The program’s current structure, while robust, 
might not fully align with the economic 
realities of the building industry in NYC. To 
enhance the law’s effectiveness and follow its 
intent, we urge elected officials to reevaluate, 
expand, and fine-tune designated zones for 
"Very Large" projects (150+ units), along with 
the implementation of more detailed, and 
varying Area Median Income (AMI) levels 
and corresponding wage requirements better 
tailored to each neighborhood’s specific 
economic conditions. Adding a new bracket 
within the program for "Mega Projects" could 
also make an impact in driving large-scale 
housing development.
We also suggest that projects under 485-x 
qualify as Type-2 actions under SEQRA.
If we don’t enhance and improve the current 
program, the city is already seeing — and 
expected to have a surge in — developments of 
exactly 99 housing units (which avoids many 
regulatory requirements), at a time when New 
Yorkers need as many homes as we can get.

What else can we do?

Fast-Track Environmental Reviews: 
Streamline environmental assessments 
for new housing and housing-related 
infrastructure to expedite development 
and avoid unnecessary delays in housing 
production. It’s common knowledge that 
increasing urban housing density reduces 
our reliance on auto travel, and thus, is 
overall beneficial for the environment. 
So long as a project follows established 
environmental standards, there’s no reason 
for an environmental review to delay 
something that is generally well known as 
being positive for the environment long 
term.
•	
Zoning for Affordable Housing: Enable as-
of-right construction of affordable housing 
in districts where less than 10% of housing 
is available at below-market rents.
•	
Deepen zoning exemptions within Special 
Transit Land Use Districts citywide: 
Expand the area to a full half mile in each 
transit zone, as set out in the original City 
of Yes proposal. 
•	
Suburban Senior Housing: State lawmakers 
must prioritize the development of senior 
housing to accommodate the rising need 
for accessible, reasonably priced living 
options for older adults. This minimizes 
the potential financial impact on municipal 
educational systems by not increasing 
student enrollments, while freeing up more 
housing overall as seniors relocate. As older 
adults retire and their needs change, it’s 
essential that they’re able to continue living 
in the communities they call home.
•	
NYC Affordability Fund: City Comptroller 
Mark Levine has called for a new ‘NYC 
Affordability Fund’ that would immediately 
deploy roughly $2.5 billion in underused 
pension capital to help finance up to 
75,000 affordable homes.
•	
Commercial Space Conversion: In recent 
years, Office-to-Residential conversions 
have been a major success story. New 
policy changes have truly made an 
impact. Changes within the City of Yes 
combined with the new 467-m incentive 
have been a game changer for office 
conversions. Projects are advancing, with 
owners securing approvals and lining up 
financing. The framework is working. 
We support maintaining and expanding 
these critical incentives to meet pressing 
housing demands and create more 24/7 
neighborhoods.
INTERBOROUGH EXPRESS
Expand upon the once-in-a-generation 
opportunity to rezone around the 
transformative new transit line, the 
Interborough Express (IBX) and create 
an “IBX Transit-Oriented Development

entire swath of land within half a mile from

IBX route. In an earlier Building Congress 
report: Housing and the

we estimated that a

rezoning around this

improvements.

Policy Best Practices from Other States: 
•	
Massachusetts model for TOD: The 
MBTA Communities law (Zoning Act 
§3A) requires cities and towns served by 
the Massachusetts Bay Transportation 
Authority (MBTA) to allow multifamily 
developments by right within a half-mile of 
transit, at a minimum gross density of 15 
units per acre. New York has no comparable 
statewide TOD mandate. New York can 
adopt a §3A-style framework that sets 
even higher base densities than 15 units per 
acre, in order to create housing where we 
need it most. New York should replicate 
this policy at a scale that best reflects New 
York’s TOD needs. 
•	
Rhode Island has launched the first wave 
of programs funded by its $120 million 
housing bond, releasing $81 million in 
allocations to support projects such as 
infrastructure upgrades for new housing, 
small-scale affordable rental and home-
repair initiatives, and revitalization of vacant 
properties. Together, these funds aim to 
clear early-stage bottlenecks, seed smaller 
infill, and keep existing affordable stock 
online.
•	
Florida’s Live Local Program Tax Credit 
was enacted in 2023 to support Florida’s 
Housing Finance Corporation State 
Apartment Incentive Loan (SAIL) initiative, 
which provides low interest loans to 
developers building affordable housing. 
With this act in place, taxpayers can 
make private contributions to the Florida 
Housing Finance Corporation and will 
receive a dollar-for-dollar state level tax 
credit.

5 Key Policy Recommendations
1.	 Speed up change orders and payments: 
Make timely processing of change orders 
and invoices standard practice, especially 
when work is complete. Build a formal 
feedback loop with the building industry, 
led by Building Congress members, to 
diagnose bottlenecks and keep projects 
moving.
2.	 Right-size risk to broaden competition: 
Rebalance risk allocation so more qualified 
firms bid on MTA work. Streamline or 
eliminate overly burdensome requirements 
where appropriate, including insurance, 
indemnification, and bonding terms that do 
not improve outcomes.
3.	 Robust Qualifications-Based Selection (QBS): 
A sound QBS process for MTA 
procurements can ensure that vital projects 
are awarded based on the expertise of the 
firm, rather than solely the lowest bid. 
This approach can bring higher-quality 
outcomes and reduce overall project costs 
by minimizing change orders and delays. 
While qualifications screening is often 
used, a stronger QBS approach would 
give technical merit greater weight in 
the final decision, supported by clear and 
transparent scoring criteria.
4.	 Accelerate station accessibility: 
Commit to full ADA compliance across the 
MTA network. Prioritize high-ridership and 
transfer hubs, publish clear timelines, and 
track progress publicly.
5.	 Use transit-oriented development to 
support transit: 
Expand tools like Zoning for Accessibility 
and similar bonuses so private development 
helps deliver accessibility upgrades while 
adding much-needed housing near stations.

New York State has ambitious goals to 
decarbonize its energy network into cleaner, 
more sustainable systems for generations. 
Namely, the State’s aspirations under the 
Climate Leadership and Community Protection 
Act (CLCPA) passed in 2019. 
Bold goals are critically important, but we must 
also acknowledge the gap between the goals 
and the realities we face. Data centers, AI hubs, 
other electricity intensive projects, and the 
fact that the average consumer is using more 
energy today than ever before, are exponentially 
adding strain to a grid that is already struggling 
to keep the lights on. In downstate New York, 
up to 90% of that electricity is generated 
using natural gas. It’s projected that our energy 
demand will double by 2050, while at the same 
time we’re decommissioning twice as many fossil 
fuel generation facilities as we’re replacing with 
renewable energy. This math simply doesn’t 
work and is creating an ever-shrinking reliability 
gap that can only be addressed with all-of-the-
above strategy that puts reliability first.  
We cannot have another blackout in New York. 
Several critical barriers are impeding progress. 
Key among these are the hurdles in the 
permitting and siting processes, which often 
delay project initiation and completion. Another 
significant obstacle is the inadequacy of current 
transmission infrastructure, which is essential for 
the efficient distribution of renewable energy.
As such, the state must:
•	
Increase Investments in Renewable Energy 
Projects: New York must considerably 
increase funding to kickstart renewables 
(including onshore and offshore wind 
projects), taking into consideration the 
ultimate cost to customers. This should also 
include a serious effort to turn Rikers Island 
into a renewable energy hub, following the 
closure of its jail facilities.
•	
Establish a Direct Renewable Energy Fund: 
Shift funding from utility ratepayers to 
a state-financed renewable fund within 
the state budget. Specifically, two recent 
legislative proposals offer new avenues for 
recurring revenue streams which could be 
used to directly pay for energy upgrades 
from our state budget, rather than through 
the Public Service Commission:
•	
S.8518 (Krueger / Kelles) - Imposes 
an excise tax on large proof-of-work 
crypto miners and directs the revenue 
into New York’s Energy Affordability 
Programs, directly funding utility bill 
relief and energy upgrades.
•	
S.8540 (Gonzalez) - Creates a 
separate utility class for large data 
centers and similar high-demand users 
so they cover the full cost of the grid 
upgrades and operations needed to 
serve them.

3D Underground (3DU) Map 
In November 2025, NYC launched the 3D 
subsurface mapping initiative, 3DU. This new 
initiative has the potential to save billions of 
dollars through efficiency improvements, and 
it is critical to ensure momentum continues 
under Mayor Mamdani’s administration. Today, 
every time a project team digs underground, 
they risk discovering unexpected utilities, voids, 
soil conditions, and other miscellaneous issues 
that do not show up in any records. When one 
party uncovers this information, it is not always 
consistently captured and shared with the next 
team working on the same site. As a result, 
designers and builders must undertake multiple 
test pits, site investigations, and one-off utility 
coordination efforts on every job. This is costly, 
time-consuming, a major source of delay and 
change orders, and impacts all New Yorkers.
Our recommendation is to fully build out 
3DU as a central, City-run repository where 
every underground “touch point” from capital 
projects and permitted work is stored and 
continually updated. An engineering-level image 
of subsurface conditions along a corridor would 
reduce the need to repeatedly “rediscover” the 
same information. Underground data should 
be stored in BIM-compatible, cross-platform 
formats that support three-dimensional modeling 
and direct use in design coordination, construction 
planning, and long-term asset management. 
Subsurface utility data should generally meet 
or exceed Quality Level B as defined by the 
American Society of Civil Engineers 38-22 
standard. With sustained support from the new 
administration, 3DU can become a core capital 
reform that saves significant taxpayer dollars that 
can be redirected to other priorities.
