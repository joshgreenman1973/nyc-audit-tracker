July 01, 2025

The New York City fiscal year 2026 budget agreement is unaffordable, unprepared for federal cuts, and underinvests in the Rainy Day Fund despite strong current year revenues. (See our statement from Friday, June 27.)

The following seven statistics provide insight into the choices New York City's leaders made in adopting the fiscal year 2026 budget.

$122.7 billion—the total fiscal year 2026 spending obligation once adjusted for prepayment of fiscal year 2026 obligations and underbudgeted programs and expenses.

Prepaying bills is common City practice, but it merely shifts timing and does not reduce a fiscal year's obligations. Furthermore, spending for overtime, CityFHEPS housing vouchers, and other programs is not fully accounted for and is included by CBC to provide a more accurate estimate of spending.

7.6 percent—the City-funded spending increase from fiscal year 2025 to fiscal year 2026, once adjusted for prepayments and underbudgeted expenses. 

This is more than double the rate of inflation and follows spending that increased by an average of 6 percent annually over the last three years.

Over $3 billion—the amount the fiscal year 2026 budget is short to pay for likely expenses and planned programs.

Overtime, CityFHEPS, public assistance, and special education are among the underbudgeted expenses. Additional analysis will lead to a refined estimate in the coming month.

$8 billion to $10 billion—the size of future budget gaps, once adjusted for underbudgeted programs.

The City underbudgets programs in the future, including the costs to comply with the State’s class size reduction mandate. 

$610 million—the amount that fiscal year 2025 spending obligations exceeded fiscal year 2025 revenues.

The City used revenues rolled forward from past years, primarily the 2021 and 2022 finance boom, to pay for its fiscal year 2025 obligations. The City is spending more than it currently brings in and can only do this because of past good times—a bad sign for the future.

$0—the amount added to the Rainy Day Fund.

Given the strength of fiscal year 2025 tax revenues, CBC recommended depositing at least $2 billion into the Fund to help preserve services and stability during the next recession.

$0—the amount set aside to mitigate the impact of federal cuts.

The magnitude, programmatic specifics, and timing of the cuts are still uncertain. But the budget did not put aside any funds to mitigate the initial blow—to preserve some services as the City determines what it will do over time to absorb the recurring and growing fiscal impacts. 

By Chris Henrichson

Director of City Studies
