January 15, 2025

The State and City Comptrollers, Independent Budget Office (IBO), and City Council all recently updated their revenue forecasts for fiscal years 2025 and 2026 to recognize billions of additional tax dollars. This increased revenue will help Mayor Eric Adams’ Administration close a portion of next year’s $5.5 billion reported budget gap, which may well be larger when accounting for underbudgeted expenses and migrant savings. 

Still, two fundamental questions remain to be answered in Mayor Adams’ Preliminary Budget:

Will the budget’s proposals be fiscally sound and stabilize the City’s budget in the long run, or continue the precarious and short-sighted path of unaffordable spending that makes future budget gaps even larger?
Will the budget estimates be accurate or will they continue to distort the City’s fiscal reality?

Although conservative revenue forecasts are a hallmark of prudent budgeting, so too are accurate spending estimates for the cost of services. The integrity of recent budgets has been undermined by the increasing trend to substantially underbudget the cost of many programs, such as uniformed overtime, housing vouchers, and public assistance, among others. This hides the true cost of operations, artificially shrinking the City’s budget gaps. 

When Mayor Adams delivers the Fiscal Year 2026 Preliminary Budget, the Citizens Budget Commission (CBC) will identify if the City provides accurate and reasonable estimates of future revenues and expenses and takes the necessary steps to ensure spending is affordable in fiscal year 2026 and beyond.

CBC will be focused on these key questions:

1. How much is the tax revenue forecast increased? The City is likely to increase projected tax revenues, but the question remains by how much. Independent fiscal monitors projected higher fiscal year 2026 tax revenues: the State Comptroller ($2.4 billion), City Comptroller ($2.1 billion), City Council ($2.0 billion), Financial Control Board ($1.6 billion), and IBO ($773 million).1 Tax revenue forecasts for fiscal years 2027 and 2028 were also higher by an average of $1.7 billion and $2.2 billion, respectively, across the fiscal monitors.

2. Does the budget accurately reflect costs of underbudgeted programs and how much underbudgeting remains in the Preliminary Budget? In recent years, the City has increasingly underbudgeted programs, such as uniformed overtime, housing vouchers, and foster care. CBC estimates the City currently underbudgets spending by $2.0 billion in fiscal year 2025 and $3.6 billion in fiscal year 2026. The City’s fiscal monitors similarly estimate fiscal year 2025 is underbudgeted by $1.3 billion to $2.8 billion, while fiscal year 2026 is short between $1.3 billion and $3.9 billion.

3. How much does the budget reduce spending for asylum seekers and migrants? In November, the City reduced the fiscal year 2025 budget for services to asylum seekers and migrants by $436 million, to $4.4 billion. With the continued decline in the number of people in the City’s care, CBC estimates additional asylum seeker savings of $1.2 billion in fiscal year 2025 and $1.8 billion in fiscal year 2026, assuming the 3 percent monthly population decline continues. The City Comptroller, State Comptroller and IBO all estimated asylum seeker savings of approximately $1.2 billion in fiscal year 2025, while their estimates for fiscal year 2026 ranged between $1.1 billion and $2.4 billion.

4. How much does the City save from vacant positions? The City had 19,529 vacant full-time and full-time equivalent positions as of September 2024, which was 6 percent of total budgeted positions. Although the City’s November Plan already reflected savings of $168 million in fiscal year 2025 due to these vacancies, the fiscal monitors estimate possible additional savings between $400 million and $1.4 billion.

5. What new programs and initiatives does the City propose to fund and how are they paid for? The Preliminary Budget will also include new programs and initiatives. These costs need to be paid for with higher revenues or offsetting savings, otherwise they will widen future budget gaps. 

For example, the City of Yes for Housing Opportunity agreement included $5 billion in additional spending. Of this, nearly $1 billion are operating costs that should be reflected in the Preliminary Budget; the remaining City costs should be reflected in the Preliminary Capital Commitment Plan.2 Mayor Adams’ proposal to eliminate the NYC Personal Income Tax for some low-income New Yorkers is expected to cost $63 million.3 Other new initiatives, such as $650 million to address street homelessness and $163 million to serve at-risk youth announced during the State of the City, should be funded in the budget as well.4

6. How, besides increased revenue, does the City close the fiscal year 2026 gap? The Preliminary Budget is required by law to be balanced in fiscal years 2025 and 2026. Higher revenue and lower spending projections will contribute to closing the gap. Two other sources can provide gap-closing resources. First, the City has $1.45 billion of in-year budgetary reserves in fiscal year 2025 to help close future budget gaps if this money is not needed to fund underbudgeted expenses in fiscal year 2025. Second, the City Comptroller estimates that $862 million was budgeted but not needed to pay for bills from prior years (“prior year payables”). This is more than double the typical $400 million in prior year payable savings.5

7. How large are the City’s out-year budget gaps and are they increased by new spending? The November Plan reports budget gaps totaling $5.6 billion in fiscal year 2027 and $6.3 billion in fiscal year 2028, although the gaps exceed $8 billion annually after accounting for underbudgeting. Mayor Adams did not ask agencies to submit budget reductions for the Preliminary Budget, so significant out-year spending reductions are unlikely. 

8. Does the City use its higher-than-projected revenues to wisely add money to the Rainy Day Fund? The City currently holds $2.0 billion in the Revenue Stabilization Funds (RSF, also referred to as the Rainy Day Fund), which is insufficient given the average recession would sap $11 billion in revenue over two years.6 CBC recommended mandatory deposits to the RSF in fiscal year 2025 would be $635 million based on the current OMB tax forecast and $1.7 billion based on the City Comptroller’s tax forecast.7

9. How is the City preparing for federal changes? Changes in federal fiscal, tax, economic, immigration, and regulatory policy will affect New York City’s finances and economy. Federal aid comprises more than $9.6 billion (or 8 percent) of the City’s fiscal year 2025 budget, particularly for education and social services. The City should prepare by proactively assessing potential risks and opportunities but should not take pre-emptive actions.
