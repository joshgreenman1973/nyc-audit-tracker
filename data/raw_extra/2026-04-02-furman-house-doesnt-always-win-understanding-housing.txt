Executive Summary
In 2024, Housing Connect distributed roughly 10,000 units across 300 buildings. Approximately  
6 million applications were filed for those buildings.1 Because new income-restricted rental units are a scarce 
and highly sought-after resource, the City allocated many of these units by lottery, specifically through 
a digital platform called NYC Housing Connect that launched in 2013 and was last updated in 2020.2 
The City’s Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD) says the median time to approve 
an applicant through the lottery process was 142 days in Fiscal Year 2025, or approximately 4.7 months, 
which is down from more than 190 days, or approximately 6.3 months, in 2023 and 2024 and 176 days 
in Fiscal Year 2022.3 Still, as the City’s affordable housing shortage has intensified, so has scrutiny of 
the process HPD uses to allocate the limited supply of new income-restricted units. 
A new administration has a set of opportunities within its control to make the lottery system work better 
and to leverage data to drive pipeline planning and engage applicants that need housing based on their 
preferences. Indeed, on January 1, 2026, Mayor Zohran Mamdani issued Executive Order 5, establishing 
an interagency task force to propose reforms that would streamline administrative processes, like NYC 
Housing Connect, that may be slowing down the construction and allocation of income-restricted units.4 
Despite legal and administrative capacity constraints on HPD’s reform efforts, we believe that there are 
potential changes that could improve the housing lottery system. To help policymakers in this effort, we 
examine how Housing Connect allocates affordable housing, using a combination of public documents 
and anonymized data from software used by marketing agents.

First, we use a combination of public documents and detailed anonymous data to lay out the marketing 
process for new income-restricted units in New York City. Then, using anonymized applicant data 
from software that marketing agents use to review applicants, we analyze a sample of approximately 
64,000 applications as they progressed through the lotteries for 101 buildings, starting from when each 
application received a log number. These data focus on batches of applicants as they go through the 
verification and offer steps with a marketing agent; our analysis does not cover appeals, complaints, or 
HPD’s audit or review of applicants’ submitted documents. We find that:
	■ The process is too long. The median time to complete the verification and offering process 
and be approved is 118 days. Combined with the up to 76 days that pass between when a building’s 
lottery opens and when applicants receive a log number and the unknown number of days it takes to 
complete lease-signings, audits, appeals, and complaints, the length of the process almost certainly 
contributes to delays occupying units for a portion of the applicants.
	■ Applicants and marketing agents waste effort in the processing stage, and the vast majority of applicants 
will not make it to the end of the verification and offer process. We observe that:
	■ Approximately 49,000 applicants (just under 77%) will end up in a “rejected” status , either 
through attrition (including not being meaningfully engaged) or explicit rejection (generally due 
to a failure to provide documentation or being found ineligible).
• Approximately 50% (nearly 32,000) do not appear to engage significantly with the verification process.
• 27% (roughly 17,000) were rejected after “engaging meaningfully” with the verification process. 
These applicants spent a median time of 29 days in the documentation stage before being rejected.
	■ Approximately 5,000 applicants in total  (3,000 before engaging meaningfully with verification 
and 2,000 after), or 8% of the total pool, withdrew their application.
	■  Only about 9,000 applicants—roughly 15% of all applicants, or just under a third of those who 
meaningfully engaged in verification—were ultimately approved.
	■  Of those approved, approximately half (52%) did not have a unit attached to their application. We 
assume that this result represents some attrition between approval and lease-signing, the placement 
of some households on priority waitlists, and instances where a unit was not available or the applicant 
was later found to be ineligible.

The most significant problem our analysis reveals is the tremendous number of applicants who drop out 
of the verification process or are rejected. This inefficiency wastes the time and hopes of the ineligible 
or never engaged applicant, the eligible applicants next in line, the marketing agents, and the owner 
with units sitting vacant. 
The evidence suggests that the current design of Housing Connect creates some of the delays in filling 
units, which raises the overall cost—in both time and money—of providing affordable housing. Each 
month that a completed affordable unit sits vacant is a waste of a valuable, publicly subsidized resource. 
Less visible, but also important, are the costs a complex and burdensome process imposes on applicants, 
marketers, developers, and the City’s housing agencies before a building is even ready to be occupied.
Proposed Reforms
After a summary of academic literature and other relevant content, the paper concludes by detailing a 
set of proposed reforms. First, we propose a set of specific reforms to the design of Housing Connect and 
the rules in the Marketing Handbook that could be implemented in the short-term.
In the short-term, automating the process and moving some of the eligibility and matching work earlier 
using structured data would help ensure the verification process is focused on a more limited set of 
applicants who have been pre-screened and have expressed a preference for the specific type of unit 
and location available. To address this problem, we propose two reforms:
	■ Applicants should apply for types of units, not buildings, and should not be able to apply for 
types of units for which they are “apparently ineligible”. This change would help structure the 
verification process around applicants who have applied for types of units for which they are eligible 
and for units that are a good match for their preferences based on self-reported information. A smaller 
set of eligible applicants would then be added to a tailored “queue” for a review of their eligibility for 
the type of unit they are more likely to accept. 
	■ HPD should expand the information collected in the “My Household” profile about applicant’s 
preferences. Housing Connect should ask more detailed, required questions about applicants’ 
preferences. The data should then be used by marketing agents to structure their lease-up process 
around applicants’ stated preferences earlier in the verification process. Applicants and marketing 
agents could determine early-on whether the unit being offered is a good match before effort is expended.

Also in the short-term, we suggest changes to the Marketing Handbook to shorten and condense 
certain stages, to provide minimum and maximum ranges for each stage and standards for 
extensions from those maximums . This will help anchor the process for marketing agents and 
applicants. Examples of timing constraints that could be amended in the Marketing Handbook include, 
but are not limited to:
	■ Shortening the length of time when lotteries are open and accepting applications (currently 60 days);
	■ Requiring that applicants be provided clear constraints on the time they have to respond to the request 
for verification documents (currently applicants must respond within 10 days) and a maximum amount 
of time by which they must have submitted a complete set of documents (currently a minimum of 
5 extra days); 
	■ Reducing marketing agents’ discretion over extending deadlines for applicants to provide verification 
documents by enumerating objective standards agents must adhere to when considering an extension. 
Second, we propose a set of larger structural reforms to the documentation process. We propose that 
the City actively work to integrate its systems that host household composition and income information 
for the purpose of calculating incomes across HPD, NYCHA and DSS. The best structural, long-term change 
would be to have all levels of government—city, state and federal—work to reconcile documentation 
and income verification requirements so the rules are the same across all means-tested subsidy 
programs (including for the Section 8 program if those rules will serve as the City’s foundational process 
for verifying incomes in Housing Connect). Then, that income calculation could be used for a period 
of time to determine whether an applicant is eligible for a range of programs, including an income-
restricted unit in Housing Connect. 
That is an enormous undertaking that cannot be accomplished by HPD alone. But it should be an aspiration 
of the City and on the agenda for discussion with the state and federal governments. In the meantime, the 
City can begin to support automating the income verification process across programs, whether 
by developing its own in-house capabilities to automate the reading of documentation or by 
incentivizing technology solutions that marketing agents could use. For example, the City could 
lead pilot projects, sponsor innovation grants, create more automatable application APIs, or develop AI 
and automation standards and guidelines.

Finally, our last proposal is broader, moving beyond the allocation of existing income-restricted units 
to exploring how the City could use Housing Connect data to expand and change the overall supply of 
income-restricted housing. By leveraging Housing Connect data on neighborhood preferences, unit 
size preferences and other variables, HPD could detail out for the public the geography of demand for 
income-restricted housing and the designs and unit sizes preferred by applicants. HPD could also enable 
applicants struggling to obtain a unit to opt-in to get involved in the community engagement process 
when the City is siting and approving affordable housing in their preferred neighborhoods.
The paper also has a section that discusses how the City might think about adopting a “citywide” priority 
system. We give a set of justifications for such an approach and also offer important operational 
considerations for policymakers who may be thinking about this kind of shift.
Introduction
From 2010 to 2024, roughly 6,500 new units of income-restricted rental housing became available each 
year in New York City through tax incentives, loans and other subsidies, and zoning density bonuses. 5 
Some of these units are set aside for homeless households and are designated supportive housing units 
allocated through a separate process. But, because new income-restricted rental units are a scarce and 
highly sought-after resource, the City allocates the rest of the units by lottery, specifically through a 
digital platform called NYC Housing Connect that launched in 2013 and was last updated in 2020.6 In 
2024, Housing Connect distributed roughly 10,000 units across 300 buildings. Approximately 6 million 
applications were filed for those buildings.7

the scarce supply of new income-restricted units. Over the years, journalists have reported burdensome

this case, policymakers have a range of potentially conflicting objectives as they work to quickly fill units,

contributing to distrust of or dissatisfaction with the City’s affordable housing programs.

Our findings suggest that both short-term, concrete reforms and longer-term, ideational reforms are 
necessary. In the shorter term, and more tangibly, we propose changing how applicants apply for units, 
collecting more information on eligibility and preferences before the verification process, and standardizing 
timelines for verifying applicants’ eligibility. These reforms would speed up the allocation process and 
help applicants apply for units that are better matches for their needs. Some of these reforms would 
also potentially address fairness concerns by reducing marketing agents’ discretion over a large pool of 
applications. In the longer term, we see value in considering adoption of some elements of a city-wide 
lottery—assigning individuals a single, city-wide lottery number in particular—and recommend further 
analysis of the substantial tradeoffs and coordination such a transition would entail.
This paper has four sections. First, it uses a combination of public documents and a detailed analysis 
of applicant data to provide an overview of the current lottery system. Second, the paper reviews the 
potential for reform based on a survey of stakeholder interests and constraints. Third, the paper compares 
the current system to theoretical ideals from the academic literature. Finally, the paper recommends 
a set of tangible reforms to the marketing process as well as broader opportunities to further housing 
production and streamline New York City’s affordable housing application processes.

M1. Marketing the Building Lotteries
Developers initiate the lottery process by submitting a Notice of Intent when construction is approximately 
70% complete (or about seven months prior to occupancy), based on the City’s guidance.18 The City adds a 
building with income-restricted units to Housing Connect and structures a lottery for each new building 
with income-restricted units, enabling the developer and/or their marketing agent to begin marketing 
the building. Marketing consists of publishing the building description and eligibility requirements 
on Housing Connect, publishing advertisements, and completing a set of required public notifications 
based on an approved affirmative marketing plan geared towards reaching those potential tenants least 
likely to apply.19  
A2. Applying for Lotteries
Housing Connect provides information on available building lotteries and facilitates electronic lottery 
applications.20 For each available building, households can see the number of units available by unit size 
(e.g., 81 one-bedrooms and 17 studios), the household sizes permitted for each unit size (e.g. 3 people), 
the minimum and maximum annual household income (broken down by the number of people in the 
household) to be eligible for the unit, and the monthly rents for each unit size based on the income 
restriction.21 Figure 2 provides an example of the wide range of “unit types” that might be available in a 
single building.

M2b. The Offer Order
From the set of remaining “apparently eligible” applicants, the developer/marketing agent uses the randomly

preference groups: people with disabilities, people currently living in the area the building is located in 
(the “community preference” group), and municipal employees or military veterans. 30 The marketing 
agent first tries to fill certain percentages of the building’s affordable units with people who belong to 
these set-aside groups and then fills remaining units from the general applicant pool. 31 Within each 
set-aside, as well as in the general pool, New York City residents are offered units before non-residents, 
and, subject to this residency prioritization, applicants with lower log numbers are offered units before 
those with higher log numbers.32

status, and other items.33 Prior to the verification stage, but not after, changes an applicant makes to the 
household information in their Housing Connect profile will be reflected in their building application.34
Unlike actual offers, which must be made according to the above process, verification can be completed 
by applicants in parallel. Agents have discretion over how to structure the verification process. According 
to HPD, agents pull “batches” of applicants that are capped at 100 applications. Agents will often run 
multiple batches per day, but they must review and take an “action” (e.g., request documents or send

Miscellaneous Additional Processes
1. Removals and Penalties
Throughout this process, an applicant can be removed from consideration for a building’s lottery in only 
three circumstances: the applicant fails to respond in a timely manner, removes themselves from the 
process at any stage, or is deemed apparently or actually ineligible..51 There are no penalties associated 
with participating in a building’s lottery, except that if an applicant submits multiple applications for 
the same building, all of their applications will be removed from consideration except the one with the 
worst lottery number.52 Applicants who submit false or knowingly incomplete information may also be 
reported for criminal prosecution.53
2. Appeals
Any rejected applicant can submit a written appeal of their rejection to a supervisor.54 Paper applicants 
are allowed certain additional appeals. Applicants who file an appeal retain their place on the log during 
review. The Marketing Handbook does not specify how many days an applicant has to file an appeal with 
the agent or how many days an agent has to make a decision about an appeal. If the applicant’s appeal 
is rejected by the agent, it must be accompanied by written reasons, and the applicant has five days to 
subsequently submit a formal complaint to the City with documentation of the believed error.55 Whether a 
unit is held for the applicant when the complaint is pending with the City is up to the discretion of the City.

documentation and verification process is burdensome for applicants and marketing agents; (2) marketing 
agents’ decisionmaking can feel arbitrary; and allocations may be unfair; and (3) the process is inefficient, 
with units sitting vacant and the marketing process imposing unnecessary costs. 56 The goal of this report 
is to analyze whether the first and third concerns are well-founded.

Using anonymized applicant data from software that marketing agents use to process applicants, we 
analyzed a sample of approximately 64,000 applications as they progressed through the lotteries for 101 
buildings, starting from when each application received a log number. The data are useful for analyzing 
the first and third concerns mentioned above because they focus on the documentation and verification 
steps, which are perceived by policymakers to be overly burdensome and a particularly inefficient  
part of the process. 
The data are not necessarily representative of the lottery process as a whole (see Appendix). The buildings 
analyzed here are more likely to be buildings with units targeted to lower-incomes and are more likely to 
have more total units relative to the full universe of buildings that are marketed using the Housing Connect 
system. Directionally, we would hypothesize that this may mean we would expect the process to take longer 
in these buildings compared to smaller buildings or buildings targeted to middle-income households.57 
We also found that marketing agents were not always consistent in how they applied and updated statuses 
in the software. Further, statuses and their definitions changed over time, and some users may have used 
statuses improperly or not kept up with administrative data entry on a timely basis. These data do not 
cover the entire Housing Connect process, instead focusing on batches of applicants as they go through 
the verification and offer parts of the process with a marketing agent. The data do not cover appeals, 
complaints, or HPD’s audit or review of applicants’ submitted documents. 
Despite these limitations, the data speak to the experience of thousands of applicant households and 
marketing agents who were working to move through the verification process. From these data, we are 
also able to approximate the lottery process’s throughput, i.e how many applicants reach each stage of 
the verification process and how long they spend there. We find that (1) the median time to complete the 
verification and offering process and be approved is 118 days, which likely contributes to delays allocating 
units, and (2) there is significant wasted effort by applicants who go on to be rejected or to self-remove 
from the process, as evidenced by the approximately 6.2 applicants who meaningfully engage in the 
process per available unit.58

We estimate that approximately 27% of the initial 64,000 applicants (roughly 17,000) were rejected after 
“engaging meaningfully” with the verification process. We refer to this group as “engaged,” which we 
understand to entail, at a minimum, responding to the marketing agent’s outreach; most applicants in this 
group additionally began document intake and review (we estimate a lower bound of 11,000, but this number 
could be significantly higher). Document review is typically the most intensive component of verification. 
As Figure 5 shows, applicants who were ultimately approved spent a median of 43 days in the documentation 
stage, and those who were ultimately rejected spent a median of 29 days in the documentation stage.59 
Based on our conversations with people experienced with the lottery, we believe there are three primary 
reasons an applicant would respond and even start the documentation process but then not receive 
a formal final status of “rejected” or “approved” for their documents. First, they may have responded 
to the initial solicitation for documents, but by the time a marketing agent solicited the full set of 
documents, they were no longer interested. Second, they may have been unable to produce the required 
documents. Third, the marketing agent may have reviewed partial documentation, deemed it incomplete 
and then the applicant has not completed the documentation but the marketing agent has not yet 
proceeded to reject the applicant and, for whatever reason, the review was not completed before the  
building was fully occupied.

The Problems the Data Identifies
The data suggest two key issues. First, the verification process is taking too long. Second, there is wasted 
effort, as shown by the roughly 30% of applicants who will ultimately be rejected (27%) or withdraw (3%) 
despite their and their marketing agent’s engagement.
The Process Takes Too Long
A minimum of 76 days passes between when a building’s lottery opens and when applicants receive a 
log number.63 Then, as we see in this data set (which begins when applicants receive a log number), the 
median time to be verified and approved for an offer is approximately 118 days. Marketing agents begin 
the lottery process roughly six to seven months before the new building receives a temporary certificate 
of occupancy (TCO). But the median total of 194 days, or approximately 6.5 months, from lottery opening 
to approval bumps up against that six to seven month timeframe. It is important to keep in mind that this 
timeframe is the median one, but many applicants face significantly longer times (more generally, the 
data is strongly right-skewed), and it does not even include subsequent stages of the process, including 
signing leases and moving into an apartment.The data suggest that approval and lease-signing may not 
be complete for a significant percentage of applicants when the TCO is received by the developer, leaving 
a portion of the units unoccupied. 
An applicant’s preferences, interest in a building relative to another housing opportunity, household 
composition, and income are more likely to have changed the longer the process takes. A large proportion 
of applicants are dropping out of the process or are failing to respond to requests for documents and 
are getting rejected as the verification process moves forward. As the process unfolds, applicants may 
be dropping out because they have moved into another unit or because their income and preferences 
have changed over the course of several months.

As currently implemented, the application process wastes significant effort. Approximately 27% of applicants 
engage meaningfully with the process—in the form of responding to marketing agents and submitting 
documents for review—yet are ultimately rejected. This high rejection rate wastes the time of the applicant 
and agent and holds up verification of batches of applicants next in line. Applicants who do not formally

The primary constraints on reform lie with the City and its agencies that implement or interact with

and there is a correspondingly significant body of scholarly work that studies them. This work is wide-

tradeoffs between different lottery systems to optimizing the actual routines used to process applicants.

Economists’ Views on Tradeoffs
Several theoretical papers have analyzed resource allocation problems motivated by the problem of 
allocating affordable housing. These papers are distinguished by their focus on how the design of the 
allocation process shapes applicants’ incentives to choose different types of housing. Applicants often 
face a tradeoff between finding a home quickly (shorter waiting time) and receiving a home in their 
preferred type of building or neighborhood. Different rules governing how applicants make housing 
choices can change their incentives to wait for a preferred type of unit. These papers ask how the 
allocation process affects various design goals, including minimizing waste, matching applicants to 
their most desired type of unit (match quality), and giving housing to applicants with the greatest need 
(targeting). Different systems are often compared according to a utilitarian welfare criteria, as well as 
these more specific objectives.
These papers offer a few high-level insights. First, because the allocation mechanism does not directly 
affect the resources available for affordable housing, the primary way it affects scarcity is through the 
amount of time it takes to assign applicants to vacant units. 84 In principle then, reducing the burden 
and complexity of the process can decrease scarcity with minimal tradeoffs.
Second, these papers highlight a tradeoff that can arise in lotteries between spending time and resources 
to precisely target affordable housing to good matches as opposed to maximizing resources spent on 
building housing while minimizing time and resources spent on encouraging better matches. 85 For 
example, many allocation mechanisms that reduce waste to make more units available for those who 
need them often do so by encouraging applicants to accept units that aren’t ideal matches for them. 86 
Whether this outcome is desirable will often depend on how policymakers weigh these different objectives, 
as well as on the extent to which applicants differ in their preferences and needs.

Finally, economic theory provides some direct insight about the Housing Connect system and about 
alternatives and reforms to that system. Because applicants to Housing Connect draw a new lottery 
number for each building and can apply to an unlimited number of buildings, the system encourages 
applicants to apply for many buildings rather than waiting for buildings they specifically prefer. This type 
of system tends to allocate housing to a greater share of “high-need” applicants because these applicants 
are typically more willing to put in the effort to keep reapplying, but it also typically yields lower match 
quality because applicants will more likely be assigned to buildings they had less interest in. By contrast, 
a first-come, first-served waitlist or citywide (“common”) lottery encourages applicants to wait for units 
they have more interest in but also limits the ability of high need applicants to “pay with their time” for 
more opportunities to be selected. Restricting the number of buildings each applicant may apply for in 
a given year can affect the need-match quality tradeoff in a similar way.87 
Optimizing the Actual Routines Used  
to Process Applicants
Statisticians and operations researchers have likewise contributed to analyses of resource allocation, but 
their focus (under the heading of “queueing theory”) has largely been on the routines (the “queues”) used 
to process allocations. For example, queueing theorists are interested in questions like when and how 
queues can be sped up, when multiple queues are better than one, and when it’s better to have special 
track processes (e.g., fast tracks).
The literature is quite technical but offers at least two core insights for housing lotteries. First, more 
efficient queueing generally requires having information about whatever is being processed—in this 
case, an application by a household for a building—and how long processing it is likely to take.88

available about them.89 This treatment can take a variety of forms, most commonly placing an applicant 
in a specialized track, parallelizing applications, or re-routing them to a different resource. A specialized 
track, like a fast-track or slow-track, typically involves tailoring either scrutiny of the application or the 
resources available for processing an application based on predictable needs of that applicant. Parallelizing 
typically involves processing batches of applications simultaneously as if they held the same place in

Proposed Reforms
Our analysis of the current lottery system, the problems that stakeholders and our data surfaced, and 
the tradeoffs scholars have identified have led us to consider several possible reforms. First, we propose 
a set of specific reforms to the design of Housing Connect and the rules in the Marketing Handbook that 
could be implemented in the short-term. Second, we propose a set of larger reforms to the documentation 
process that would require significant structural changes. Finally, our last proposal is broader, and rather 
than being geared towards allocating the existing number of income-restricted units, details how the City 
could use Housing Connect data to expand and change the overall supply of income-restricted housing.

Re-Designing Housing Connect Elements
One of the most significant problems our analysis reveals is the tremendous number of applicants who 
drop out of the verification process or are rejected, wasting the time and hopes of the ineligible applicant

sitting vacant. In the short term, implementing reforms to automate the process and move some of the 
eligibility and matching work earlier using structured data would help ensure the verification process is 
focused on checking eligibility for a more limited set of applicants who have been screened for eligibility 
and have expressed a preference for the specific type of unit and location available.
To address this problem, we propose two reforms designed to better screen out ineligible applicants 
without overly constraining applicants’ choices: 
1. Applicants Should Apply for Types of Units, not Buildings, and Should Not Be Able to Apply 
for Types of Units for which they are “Apparently Ineligible”: Applicants should apply for types of 
unit (e.g. 1 bedrooms at 50% area median income and 2 bedrooms at 50% area median income) within 
a building, not to the building at large. Where applicants are eligible for more than one type of unit, 
they could apply for more than one type. This change will help structure the queuing process around 
types of units and ensure marketing agents are aware of a resident’s preference for a specific type of 
unit while also ensuring that residents are not applying for a building hoping to secure a type of unit 
for which they are not eligible. When a resident clicks to “apply” for a unit type, they should also not be 
able to override the prompt mentioned above and instead should be redirected to the “My Household” 
portal to make changes to their self-reported income and household composition before they apply for 
a unit for which they are apparently ineligible. This inability to override apparent ineligibility will help 
structure the verification process around applicants who have applied for types of units for which they 
are eligible and for units that are a good match for their preferences based on self-reported information. 
These changes would also help encourage applicants to keep their information up-to-date.

more detailed questions about applicants’ preferences, including making existing questions about 
pet policies and accessibility needs mandatory and adding questions about borough or neighborhood 
preferences. Applicants should also select their preferred unit size from a menu of options that is 
based on their household size and occupancy standards. Unlike income and household composition

and Condense the Time It Takes for Some 
Stages, and to Anchor Other Stages to Clear 
Expectations on Timing 
As mentioned above, the median time from when an applicant is logged to when they are approved for a 
unit is currently around 118 days in our data. Then, it takes even longer to actually sign the lease. A longer 
process means applicants are more likely to drop-out either because they move somewhere else or lose 
interest, or because contact information or household composition and income information have changed. 
Shortening timelines and providing minimum and maximum ranges for each stage, as well as standards 
for extensions from those maximums, will help anchor the process for marketing agents and applicants. 
Providing clear boundaries and guidance on how long the process should take at each stage for both 
the applicants and the marketing agents will also reduce the marketing agent’s discretion and make the 
process potentially seem less unfair and less arbitrary.
Examples of timing constraints that could be amended in the Marketing Handbook include: 
	■ Shortening the length of time when lotteries are open and applications for a new lottery can be 
accepted (currently 60 days). 
	■ Requiring that applicants be provided clear time constraints on the time they have to respond to the

These standards might include a set number of days for an extension as well as a standard set of

	■ Requiring that marketing agents review and then “reject” or “accept” documentation within a maximum

	■ Defining the amount of time that is “reasonable” between an offer being accepted and a lease being

These recommendations do involve some tradeoffs, but we see some clear advantages to our proposed 
approach. A shorter application window before a building’s lottery closes ensures applicants’ interest and 
information are more timely and more likely to be accurate in subsequent stages of the process. Likewise, 
providing clear boundaries on how long each stage (document submissions, a marketing agent’s review, 
and the agency’s reviews) should take would ensure better reporting against a performance metric. Then, 
all stakeholders could understand whether a building’s verification process is taking longer than that 
performance metric mandates and work to speed up the process. Clear timelines and objective standards 
for extensions also would limit marketing agents’ discretion. There is a perception that some marketing 
agents arbitrarily provide favorable treatment and longer extensions to some applicants than others. 
Clear timelines would advance fairness by ensuring that the process is applied uniformly. 
That said, providing applicants longer windows to apply and longer, looser windows to submit documentation 
may help expand access. Some applicants may need more time because securing and submitting 
documents is difficult and because they cannot move quickly into a new apartment. This challenge could 
be addressed in two ways. First, the City could work to provide individuals whom it believes will need 
assistance navigating the application and verification process with prompt and direct assistance from 
trained providers. Second, the City could draft the objective standards for extensions in a manner that 
makes marketing agents provide additional time to the populations it believes would be disadvantaged 
by shorter, more defined timelines.  
There is another consideration that also might weigh against these changes. If marketing agents are using 
their discretion to give extensions to applicants who are almost finished with submitting documentation, 
then tightening the timelines and limiting discretion may increase the number of applicants who fail to

Structural Changes 
Simplifying Documentation Requirements, Integrating Housing

Another significant problem confirmed by our analysis is the extended processing time that applicants 
face to verify eligibility. This timeline taxes applicants’ and marketing agents’ energy and patience and 
leaves units that could be housing New Yorkers in need vacant. 
HPD has made addressing documentation for verification a priority recently. 91 The agency published 
changes to the Marketing Handbook in April 2025 that included a safe harbor provision allowing applicants 
to submit fewer documents if they had income calculated for other federal means-tested programs and 
has advocated at the federal level for loosening federal documentation rules, like the requirement that 
documents be no older than 120 days for the LIHTC program.92 
Still, the Marketing Handbook tells users “income eligibility at admission is determined in the same way 
as it is determined under the federal Section 8 program except where the Agency specifies that additional 
requirements or restrictions apply.”93 To realize the benefit of the changes made in April 2025, the City 
should actively work to integrate its systems that host household composition and income information 
for the purpose of calculating incomes using Section 8 rules. If an applicant says they are receiving 
rental assistance (especially a voucher), the applicant’s household information in Housing Connect 
should be able to link to the applicant’s recertification process in NYCHA, DSS or HPD’s system. That 
way, the Housing Connect verification process and the voucher certification process are integrated and 
can happen in parallel; the processes would not be stuck waiting for each other during verification and

Even if that reform is implemented, the best long-term solution would be to work with the federal 
government to meaningfully simplify the income verification process. The Section 8 income calculation 
rules are an exceedingly technical and difficult method for calculating incomes. The process requires 
the agent to reconcile multiple pay stubs, make projections, and look at granular information about 
an applicant’s assets, among other tasks. Then, if a project uses low-income housing tax credits, city 
subsidies, or a tax abatement program, the applicant or the marketing agent may need to complete separate 
certifications and income verification requirements that are governed by different rules from Section 8.  
Regulations governing everything from how many days old a document can be while still remaining a 
permitted source for verification to how to treat different types of income may vary across programs. 
We recognize that reconciling the rules for calculating income across several means-tested programs is 
a thorny problem. It is perhaps thorniest in the affordable housing context, where one building may be 
layering multiple subsidy types, each with different rules on how to verify incomes. Certainly, if a building 
benefits from only a tax abatement program, it should require a less intensive eligibility process relative to 
a building that also involves federal tax credits. But adjusting the rules for some buildings and not others 
is also not a long-term solution and it may be difficult to train marketing agents on multiple processes. 
The best structural, long-term change would be to have all levels of government—city, state and federal—
work to reconcile documentation and income verification requirements so the rules are the same across 
all means-tested subsidy programs (including for the Section 8 program if those rules will serve as the 
City’s foundational process for verifying incomes in Housing Connect). The reconciled documentation and 
income verification rules should be written to be uniform, consistent and easy to apply. Most importantly, 
the goal of the rules should be to move towards using automation tools that can read documents and  
assign an income to a household easily with standard inputs. Then, that income calculation could be 
used for a period of time to determine whether an applicant is eligible for a range of programs, including 
an income-restricted unit in Housing Connect. 
This kind of change would require a very heavy lift to reconcile statutes and regulations across multiple 
levels of government, deploy tools for automation, and create a centralized system to produce an income 
calculation. That effort would be an enormous undertaking that cannot be accomplished by HPD 
alone, but it should be an aspiration of the City and on the agenda for discussion with the state and 
federal governments.

In the meantime, the City can begin to support automating the verification process, whether by developing

solutions that marketing agents could use. For example, the City could lead pilot projects, sponsor 
innovation grants, create more automatable application APIs, or develop AI and automation standards 
and guidelines. Some marketing agents are already exploring these emerging technologies, and the 
agencies should consider convening those that are to learn how the City can best provide support. 
Broader Opportunities
Additional Value of Gathering More Information About 
Applicant’s Preferences 
Though not explicit in most stakeholders’ concerns with the current lottery system, we additionally 
believe the scarcity and absence of income-restricted housing in certain neighborhoods represents a 
significant problem. To address this problem, we propose leveraging Housing Connect to increase supply 
and improve the process for siting and approving affordable housing. 
One of the short-term proposals mentioned above is for Housing Connect to solicit users’ housing 
preferences when they first create, and subsequently update, their household information. These 
preferences would include geography and the number of bedrooms they would prefer. This structured 
data set is useful to better screen applicants, but it is also a powerful tool to understand affordable housing 
demand (in the economic sense) and should be used by the City in other contexts.
	■ Pipeline Planning and Geography of Demand: Understanding demand by geography would help 
inform debates about which neighborhoods and council districts are not providing enough supply 
because they are not approving enough affordable housing to meet demand for identified applicants 
who want to live in that neighborhood as shown in Housing Connect.
	■ Pipeline Planning and Design Uses: Understanding demand as shown in Housing Connect by unit 
size (and whether applicants prefer elevator buildings or other design elements) would be a powerful 
tool to (1) justify or change designs for specific projects and to (2) inform amendments to HPD’s Design 
Guidelines or term sheets. The goal would be to encourage projects that meet the demands for identified 
applicants seeking a mix of unit sizes or amenity preferences.
	■ Constituency Building for Projects: Allowing applicants in Housing Connect to opt in or out of 
alerts from HPD about proposed, but not yet approved, projects that would produce units that meet 
their neighborhood and size preferences could be an effective tool to build a constituency for specific

projects.94 These applicants would then be able to choose whether to participate in the community 
engagement process for housing that meets their preferences. This ability to opt in to HPD alerts about 
proposed projects would help organize what is a relatively latent constituency for new affordable 
housing construction.
Conclusion
Housing Connect is a critical system, used by marketing agents, owners, HPD, and hundreds of thousands 
of applicants each year who are seeking an affordable unit in New York City. The current lottery process 
takes too long—the whole process takes a median of more than 140 days based on HPD’s calculation, and 
based on our calculation, the verification, approval and offer process alone takes a median of 118 days. 
In addition, applicants and marketing agents waste effort in the processing stage working through 
documentation for a tremendous volume of applicants who will not be approved. We found that the vast 
majority of applicants will not make it to the end of the process, either because of attrition where the 
applicant is not being meaningfully engaged or is dropping out or because of explicit rejection where 
the applicant fails to provide documentation or is found ineligible. 
This high likelihood of rejection wastes the time and hopes of the ineligible applicant, the eligible applicants 
next in line, the marketing agents, and the owner left with vacant units. The evidence suggests that the 
current design of Housing Connect creates long delays in filling units, which raises the overall cost—in 
both time and money—of providing affordable housing. Each month that a completed affordable unit 
sits vacant is a waste of a valuable, publicly subsidized resource. Less visible, but also important, are the 
costs a complex and burdensome process imposes on applicants, marketers, developers, and the City’s 
housing agencies before a building is even ready to be occupied.

Should the City Adopt a  
“Citywide Lottery” Approach?
One often-discussed reform to the lottery system would entail shifting from building-by-building lotteries 
to all buildings in a wider geographic area like a neighborhood or borough, or even to a citywide lottery 
that happens periodically. One motivation for such a change flows from applicants having very limited 
information about their chances to get housing, which tends to give applicants an incentive to apply to as 
many buildings as possible. This incentive might explain why so many applicants apply to buildings but 
later decline offers, are rejected, or drop-out of the process, all of which delays the process of filling units.
A structural shift to a citywide priority system might address some of these concerns, depending on its 
design. In this section, we discuss how such a system could be designed and some of the implementation 
challenges and questions we anticipate that would require additional analysis by policymakers. 
Examples of Similar Citywide Priority Systems 
in New York City
There are two existing examples in New York City that are structured like a citywide priority system. 
One example is the centrally managed “waiting list” system used for allocating public housing units and 
Housing Choice Vouchers citywide.95 Each of these systems maintain a central list, with households on 
the list assigned to “preference” categories based on defined criteria. When a unit or a voucher becomes 
available, a household is selected off the list based on a defined set of rules that take preference category 
and length of time on the list into account. The selected applicant then submits the documents needed 
to verify eligibility with documentation one time upon selection. Applicants for public housing thus have 
less choice with respect to their assigned unit than if they were allowed to apply separately to any building. 
Adopting a fully centralized “waiting list” system would be difficult in the Housing Connect context. 
Here, there is greater variability in who is managing the resource (as opposed to vouchers, where the 
subsidy is managed by one administrator, and public housing, where there is one entity managing the 
portfolio) and also a wide range of income-restrictions and regulatory requirements applicable to different 
buildings and each unit within a building. For that reason, coordination between marketing agents and 
developers of different buildings participating in a citywide system may be necessary.

A second example of a citywide priority system is the process used by the New York City Department 
of Education to allocate slots in schools and preschools. In this system, each year, parents of eligible 
children rank a list of schools in order of preference.96 Each account is assigned a number at random that

slots, the preference lists of all families, and the randomly assigned order number of all families and 
assigns out slots in a school to each family. Students then have the option to enroll or to stay on a waitlist 
for another school where they have an assigned order number on that waitlist. 
Adopting a fully centralized allocation system like the one used for schools would be difficult for housing 
for two reasons. First, buildings become available continuously over time, and the exact timing is hard 
to predict in advance, unlike schools’ annual cycle. Second, there are significantly fewer housing units 
available than applicants, while schools can plan to ensure there are enough slots available citywide for 
all eligible students. 
Designing a Citywide Priority System for 
Income-Restricted Units Marketed through 
Housing Connect
There are a number of important elements for policymakers if they consider using a citywide priority 
system for affordable housing. The system would need to give applicants already using Housing Connect 
a priority number immediately, and new applicants would be given a number when creating a profile. 
This number could range from 0 to 100, with a lower number corresponding to higher priority. An 
applicant with a number of 13.57, for example, would be told that approximately 13.57% of Housing 
Connect profiles have a better priority number.
From there, the system could operate similarly to current practice. Applicants could apply to any buildings 
listed on Housing Connect. When the application window for a building closed, HPD would sort applicants 
by their priority numbers and share the resulting list with the marketing agent. Marketing agents would 
screen applicants as they currently do, filling set-aside preference categories before screening remaining 
applicants in priority order.

The advantage of this approach is the effects it might have on applicant behavior. This change would 
give applicants more information about their chances of success before they apply to any buildings, 
potentially changing how or whether they decide to apply. For example:
	■ Applicants with very good priority numbers would have a good chance of being selected wherever they 
apply. We expect them to respond by applying to fewer buildings and waiting until a good fit becomes 
available on Housing Connect.
	■ Applicants with very bad priority numbers would know that they are unlikely to receive housing 
through Housing Connect. We would expect that many of these applicants will stop applying for 
Housing Connect buildings.
	■ Applicants with middling priority numbers could continue applying to any building they wish but 
would be most likely to be selected for less popular buildings.
If applicants respond in this way, we expect the following consequences:
	■ Applicants with very good priority numbers would be able to choose buildings well-suited to their needs.
	■ Applicants with bad numbers would spend less time browsing listings on Housing Connect and 
applying to buildings.
	■ The total volume of applications would decrease (due to decreased applications from applicants with 
good numbers as well as those with bad numbers). 
	■ Developers might find that the applicants they screen—most of whom would have good priority 
numbers—have intentionally chosen their building and are more likely to complete the lease-up process.
The academic literature offers theoretical evidence that a centralized priority list leads to better outcomes 
for applicants. The findings that a citywide lottery gives more applicants one of their top choices and 
that the improvements are largest in markets where applicants outnumber positions are supported by 
a paper by Arnosti and Shi (2020). 97 The authors conclude that using independent lotteries for each 
building when allocating housing units results in many low quality matches which could be avoided by 
using a citywide lottery. 
Before adopting a citywide approach, policymakers should work with existing data to measure empirically 
whether these desirable effects are likely to occur. After all, in 2024, Housing Connect distributed 
roughly 10,000 units across 300 buildings but approximately 6 million applications were filed for those 
buildings.98 With that level of scarcity and such high demand, applicants might justifiably only feel like

Details For Future Discussion
The potential benefits of a citywide lottery depend on careful consideration of implementation-related 
decisions and the many unintended consequences that might occur. We considered a range of risks that 
policymakers may want to consider before moving to such a system:
1. Applicants Altering their Behavior to Secure Better Numbers: Households would naturally realize 
the value of having a very good priority number, and might try to “game” the system (for example by 
creating many accounts and using the account with the best priority number to apply). HPD would 
have to anticipate and prevent such behavior. It is important to note here that the New York City 
Department of Education (DOE) moved away from sharing random admission priority numbers for 
3-K, pre-K and kindergarten applicants before an application is submitted in order “to ensure that 
only one random number is generated per child.”99 The DOE had previously shared these numbers 
prior to the application deadline. As we explain above, sharing the number that corresponds to an 
applicant’s chances is a critical step for achieving the change in applicant behavior that would lead 
to lower volumes and better matching. 
2. Giving Households Multiple Chances to Secure a Better Number: Policymakers likely would 
not want to give an applicant a permanently poor priority number. One alternative is to “refresh” 
all applicants’ numbers at a point in time. Another alternative is to “refresh” numbers after some 
interval has passed (for example, quarterly or one year after the previous number was assigned). 
However, it is important that the “refresh” happen infrequently enough that applicants with good 
priority numbers who are actively searching can reasonably expect to find a suitable apartment 
before their priority number changes. To minimize disruption, the “refresh” should also occur when 
applicants are not applying for buildings or are not being verified for units based on an old number. 
This outcome may be difficult to achieve in a system like Housing Connect where buildings become 
available on a rolling basis. HPD would need to consider whether it has enough information to build 
a structured process that resets on an interval. In that kind of system, applicants use new priority 
numbers every quarter or every year for a new batch of buildings that HPD expects will complete 
construction in that interval.

3. Duplicated Screening Effort: If a high priority household applies to many buildings in a short 
time period, we would expect that many developers will screen this household, resulting in wasted 
effort. During the screening process, a household with a good priority number may also hold up the 
queue as they wait for another building’s screening process to unfold or decide between multiple 
offers. If households with good priority numbers respond by applying selectively, this situation 
should be rare. Likewise, if households are restricted and can move forward with only one building 
(their first choice), this wasted effort on the part of developers would not occur. This concern could 
also be mitigated with improved sharing of information across developers to enable documents and 
findings from one developer to be applied to other buildings or with centralized management of the 
screening process by HPD. 
4. Impacts to People’s Chances of Getting Housing: In aggregate, moving to a citywide priority system 
should not change people’s chances of getting housing if the pool of applicants remained roughly 
the same. However, it would affect individual applicants’ chances. Most likely, it will lower chances 
of a successful match for applicants who currently apply to many developments while raising them 
for applicants who are currently selective.
5. Embedding Other Preferences Into the Priority Systems: HPD could determine applicants’ priority 
numbers randomly or by taking other factors into account. For example, priority could be given to 
households that have waited longest or have other particular attributes. We are not recommending 
these changes but note that HPD would have centralized control over the priority numbers assigned 
citywide and could embed other policy changes into that process over time.
