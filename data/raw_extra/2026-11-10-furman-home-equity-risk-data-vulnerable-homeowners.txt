Introduction
For many New Yorkers, homeownership represents their most significant investment and a valuable asset to pass on to the next generation. As homeowners age and home values appreciate rapidly, incomplete property records and title issues may make some homeowners vulnerable to exploitation. When ownership or other property records are unclear, residents may be more susceptible to title-related complications, legal challenges, or pressure to sell under unfavorable terms.
In partnership with a number of organizations and government agencies that work closely with homeowners, this brief leverages large data sets to track how many homeowners in New York City are at risk of title problems and other issues that may lead to a loss of equity. This analysis provides an updated look at the potential scale of the problem and the extent to which title issues overlap with other indicators of homeowner financial distress. It also provides insights on which neighborhoods may be most at risk.
This data brief builds on earlier work to better understand the risks facing long-time New York City homeowners and inform policy responses. In our 2025 brief, Using Data to Prevent the Loss of Home Equity in New York City , the NYU Furman Center estimated the number and geographic distribution of 1-3 family homes in New York City with indicators that suggested vulnerability to predatory tactics or title-related issues. Since then, the NYU Furman Center has met with government officials, legal services providers, nonprofit partners, and law enforcement officials to understand how additional risk indicators should be included in the analysis. Simultaneously, the NYU Furman Center created a restricted-access mapping tool that uses these data to help these institutions provide support to at-risk homeowners. This data brief both updates the data published last year and explains and estimates the prevalence of additional indicators that could signal homeowner risk.
In our analysis of 1-3 unit homes in New York City (excluding Staten Island), we find the following:
• The Bronx has the highest prevalence of properties at high risk of title issues, at 0.54 percent of human-owned 1-3 unit homes.
• Signs of financial distress (including being on the tax lien sale list) are seven times higher among properties at high risk of title issues than for all human-owned single-family homes.
• The percentage of all human-to-entity property transfers that were deemed to have a broken chain of title because the grantor on the new deed did not match the grantee on the previous deed or because there was no prior recorded deed reached its highest level in the last decade.
• Between 2015 and 2025, human-to-entity transfers with a broken chain of title were mostly located in eastern Queens, the north Bronx, and southeastern Brooklyn.
• Community districts with the highest number of properties that are at high risk of title issues and human-to-entity transfers with a broken chain of title also have higher shares of Black, Asian, and foreign-born residents.
Targeted outreach, legal assistance, law enforcement, and financial support for homeowners are essential to address vulnerabilities and prevent future home equity loss. It is our hope that this analysis, alongside the restricted data tool shared with partner organizations, can inform outreach efforts, track the prevalence of certain fraudulent schemes, and provide policymakers with the information needed to target their interventions most effectively. 
The analysis that follows first reviews the policy landscape and relevant recent research on New York City’s response to deed theft and title issues. It then presents our findings, focusing first on properties at high risk of title issues, second on uptake of a new estate planning tool called transfer on death deeds, and third we discuss the prevalence of flagged human-to-entity transfers. Next, we discuss data transparency considerations and conclusions from our research. Finally, we provide an appendix with details about our methodology and data analysis.
If you believe you are the victim of deed theft, the New York State Attorney General’s Office recommends that you file a complaint with law enforcement and contact an attorney. For more information, please review the resources provided by the New York State Attorney General’s Office .
Recent Policy and Research on Deed Theft and Title Issues
In recent years, policymakers have prioritized addressing and preventing deed theft, heirs’ property partition actions, and other title-related issues. In one notable example, in April of this year, Mayor Mamdani established the Mayor’s Office of Deed Theft Prevention, housed in the Department of Finance. This new office is tasked with coordinating an interagency effort to prevent, detect, investigate, and respond to deed theft. 1
Elected officials in New York have also enacted a number of laws targeting specific heirs’ property and deed theft scenarios and protecting vulnerable populations. New York’s state-level heirs’ property legislation, enacted in 2019, 2 is based on the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (UPHPA), which was approved by the Uniform Law Commission in 2010. 3
In 2024, New York State enacted the Heirs Property Protection and Deed Theft Prevention Act.  This legislation strengthened the 2019 law by expanding protections for heirs’ property owners and barring non-heir purchasers with a fractional interest in heirs’ property from initiating a partition action. The act also increased penalties for deed theft and introduced Transfer on Death Deeds (TODDs). 4 A TODD is a low-cost estate planning tool that allows property owners to designate a beneficiary to automatically inherit the property upon the owner’s death, with no need to go through probate for the transferred property. 5 Other recent state-level legislation also focuses on deed theft. 6
Alongside this continued legislative interest in protecting heirs’ property owners and preventing deed theft of vulnerable properties, recent research explores the prevalence of title issues and heirs’ property in New York City. In our 2025 brief , we drew on public and proprietary data to identify 1-3 unit residential properties with either at least one deceased owner or no recorded ownership transfers or mortgage activity since 1995. Properties that exhibited one of these indicators were flagged as “at risk,” while properties that exhibited both indicators were defined as “high risk” for title-related vulnerability. The report, which covered all of New York City except Staten Island, identified approximately 45,000 properties, worth an estimated $34.7 billion, that exhibited at least one risk indicator. A subset of approximately 3,700 properties, worth an estimated $3.0 billion, met both risk indicators. 7
In April 2026, the Center for New York City Neighborhoods analyzed claims for partition cases filed between March and July of 2024. By flagging indicator phrases (such as “partition,” “deceased,” and “heirs”) and LLC purchases, the report identified 182 likely heirs’ property partition cases with a mean value of $1.14 million per property.  The Center estimated that approximately 350 heirs’ property partition lawsuits are filed annually in New York City. 8
Findings
Title-Related Vulnerabilities
This section focuses on properties that are at high risk of title-related vulnerabilities (including heirs’ property issues 9 and cases where title may be unclear).
When a title is unclear, it can be difficult for owners to lay claim to and fully leverage the value of their home. A clear title is often required to use the property as collateral for a loan, enroll in tax relief programs, or otherwise use the home as a financial asset, and properties with unclear title cannot receive FEMA disaster recovery funds or enroll in many home repair programs.
Additionally, given New York City’s tight real estate market and price appreciation over time, many of these properties have significantly increased in value since they were purchased. Aging longtime residents or recent inheritors of a fractional interest in the property may not know the current market value of their home and, because of unclear title, they may be more vulnerable to scams when presented with lower offers from a predatory actor.
We analyzed the prevalence of properties with unclear title, classifying properties as “high risk” if (1) an owner of record is deceased, and (2) either no deed was recorded against the property at all, or the most recent deed is at least 30 years old (from 1996 or earlier). 10 We note that these homes are not necessarily “heirs’ property,” but they are at a higher risk of having unclear title and likely require an intervention to stabilize the property’s ownership. 
We then examined whether these properties also had other indicators of financial distress, including being on the tax lien sale list or having property tax debts that exceeded certain thresholds. We find that financial distress indicators are much more prevalent among the high-risk properties with unclear titles than among all 1-3 unit residential properties with a human owner.
The Bronx and Queens have the highest prevalence of properties at high risk of having unclear titles.
Table 1 shows the number and percentage of properties at high risk of title issues citywide and within each New York City borough, excluding Staten Island. We find a total of 2,300 high-risk properties in New York City. 11 Over 50 percent of the city’s high-risk properties are located in Queens; however, the Bronx has the highest share of 1-3 unit properties that are at high risk (0.52% compared to 0.54%, respectively). Manhattan has both the lowest number and share of high-risk properties, which is largely driven by the fact that the borough has fewer 1-3 family homes with human owners.

Properties at high risk of title issues are seven times more likely to appear on the tax lien sale list or have delinquent property taxes than all human-owned single family homes.
We find that properties at a high risk for unclear title issues are also more likely to exhibit other indicators of financial distress, highlighting the vulnerability of the owners of these homes. For example, compared to the full universe of human-owned 1-3 unit properties, the high-risk properties are almost seven times more likely to be included on the tax lien sale list in 2025 (0.24% compared to 1.57%) for property tax debt, water and sewer debt, and other related fees and charges. 12 In addition, 12.87 percent of high-risk properties owed more than $500 in property taxes at the beginning of Fiscal Year 27, compared to just 1.93 percent of all 1-3 unit residential properties with human owners.
The total number of 1-3 unit properties owing more than $500 in property taxes has been relatively stable over the last several years. However, the 2026 total of 8,618 properties is the lowest number since the COVID-19 pandemic. At the end of Fiscal Year 2021, we found 8,937 properties owing more than $500, and then the number increased by 5.4 percent, peaking at 9,421 properties in Fiscal Year 2022. Last year, at the end of Fiscal Year 2025, we found 9,276 properties that owed more than $500 in property taxes.

Transfer on Death Deeds
Transfer on Death Deeds are an important new tool in estate planning that became available in 2024 as part of the Heirs Property Protection and Deed Theft Prevention Act. During our focus groups, practitioners noted that it would be useful to track uptake of this option, especially among homeowners at high risk of having unclear titles.
When an owner records a TODD, heirs will directly inherit a property without having to go through probate upon the owner’s death. This form of estate planning is a low-cost way to ensure that a property passes directly and securely to designated beneficiaries, avoiding potentially complicated and expensive court proceedings and helping to prevent tangled title issues.
Uptake of Transfer on Death Deeds has risen since their introduction in 2024, though the overall volume is low.
Figure 1 shows the number of TODDs recorded across New York City and its boroughs, not including Staten Island, on a monthly basis, from the introduction of TODDs in July 2024 to July 2026. In the second half of 2024, 181 TODDs were recorded citywide; over 2025, a total of 713 TODDs were filed. Between January and July 2026, 486 TODDs had been filed. It is not immediately clear why recorded TODDs spiked in some spring or summer months in 2025 and 2026. Note that, in our prior work, we found approximately 45,000 properties for which there was no recorded deed or mortgage activity since 1995 or at least one deceased owner was still listed as an owner of record. Based on those indicators, we might assume the homeowners for those 45,000 properties are older or that the home was, for example, previously passed to an aging spouse. Though still underinclusive of the overall need, this data point still suggests there may be a substantial number of homes in need of further outreach about the TODD option.
At a borough level, TODD uptake follows the distribution of 1-3 unit properties, with Queens and Brooklyn recording far more TODDs than Manhattan. TODD numbers may rise as legal services organizations and government officials continue to introduce this new estate planning option to homeowners.

Human-to-Entity Transfers
This section introduces new scenarios and a different but related set of issues identified by the practitioners in our focus groups—transfers from a human owner to an entity (including an LLC) that could signal homeowner vulnerability. Not all human-to-entity property transfers reflect suspicious activity, but certain types of human-to-entity transfers may signal that a scam or fraudulent scheme targeting vulnerable homeowners has already taken place, including after a tangled title or heirs’ property issue. 
Informed by focus group conversations, we reviewed and flagged two types of human-to-entity transfers:
• Grantor/Grantee Mismatch: We flagged human-to-entity transfers where the name of the grantor (seller) to an entity did not match the name of the listed grantee (buyer) on the property’s previous deed, such as cases where the seller in a 2026 transaction is not the same as the buyer in a prior deed, causing an apparent break in the chain of title. Focus group participants indicated that this gap in the documented ownership chain could indicate that a bad actor has transferred fractional interests to an LLC, or that another type of scam has taken place. This category may also capture other types of transfers, such as a referee’s deed post-foreclosure. 
• No Prior Deed: We also identified human-to-entity transfers where the transferred property had no deed on record before the deed that transferred ownership to the entity. As with the previous category, this identified gap in documentation raises red flags.
We also analyzed all human-to-entity transfers across each community district to understand how many of these transfers occur, trends in this practice over time, and where these transfers are most common. 
The total number of all human-to-entity transfers in New York City dropped sharply in 2020, likely due to pandemic disruptions, and has since increased but remains below pre-pandemic levels.
In the five years before the pandemic, there were between 5,500 and 6,500 human-to-entity transfers each year. The number of transfers dipped to just 4,246 in 2020, but has been slowly increasing since then (with a slight dip in 2023). In 2025, there were more than 6,000 human-to-entity transfers for the first time since the pandemic. Importantly, human-to-entity transfers on their own are not an indicator of predatory activity and may be legitimate sales.

Data Transparency and Other Considerations
Our analysis tracks indicators of vulnerability for property owners, including risk of title issues and deed theft. Based on focus group conversations with stakeholders, we were able to identify a number of such scenarios in the data. However, those conversations also surfaced a number of scenarios and indicators that we cannot identify given currently available data. To gain more insight into these scenarios, we recommend updating the publicly readable data to specify the deed type and whether properties go through Surrogate’s Court. In addition, it would be helpful to collect machine-readable information on the notaries or attorneys who presented the recorded document so we could identify patterns and help determine whether specific networks or actors are repetitively engaging in suspicious conduct. In order to avoid compromising the effectiveness of these recommendations, we do not outline them in detail here. We will share our recommendations for improving data transparency directly with the relevant agencies.
We created our interactive map and dashboard detailing at-risk households to assist government officials, legal service providers and law enforcement in identifying the scale of the issue at a neighborhood level. The protected map includes property-level information to help partners target their outreach. As we outlined in our 2025 brief, Using Data to Prevent the Loss of Home Equity in New York City , that outreach should be performed by trusted organizations that have deep local relationships with the communities that we have identified as at risk. Local organizations can help ensure that outreach is also carried out in a multilingual and culturally competent manner. Outreach can inform homeowners of the need for estate planning (including tools like TODDs), warn them of scams, and connect them with trusted legal providers. Furthermore, outreach can direct homeowners to programs that provide financial support for property taxes, water charges, home repairs, and disaster relief.
Directing homeowners to current resources and programs, while effective in many ways, is not enough to protect vulnerable homeowners. For a more comprehensive discussion of policy recommendations and data considerations that can help protect at-risk homeowners, see our 2025 brief.
Conclusion
Our analysis leverages updated data to better understand the scale and geography of properties that are vulnerable to or may have experienced equity stripping, a topic we first explored in our 2025 brief. We identify thousands of properties at risk of tangled title issues. Those properties also show heightened signs of financial distress, raising concerns about underlying challenges these homeowners are facing. High-risk properties are seven times more likely to show up on the City’s most recent tax lien sale list and six times more likely to be delinquent on property taxes than all 1-3 unit properties owned by individuals. In an encouraging sign, we find that 1,380 Transfer on Death Deeds that can help lower homeowner risk have been filed between July 2024, when the tool was implemented, and July 2026. 
Across four focus groups with legal services providers, city government leaders, nonprofit partners, and law enforcement agencies, we consistently heard about types of human-to-entity property transfers that raise concerns about potential scams or other schemes targeting homeowners. Our analysis shows that these transfers have risen over the last few years. They are heavily concentrated in a few neighborhoods, including Jamaica/Hollis and Queens Village in Queens. Similar to high-risk properties, we find that properties with human-to-entity transfers where there is a gap in the chain of title are also much more likely to have an indicator of financial distress. Finally, neighborhoods with the highest concentrations of flagged transfers also have higher shares of Black, Asian, and foreign-born residents than other parts of the city.
Our data show that the risk of unclear title issues remains significant, and the data also help identify the most vulnerable neighborhoods so our partners can better target resources and outreach. The same data underlying this analysis are currently available in a restricted, detailed dashboard and mapping tool for our partner organizations engaging in critical legal assistance and enforcement activities. By surfacing patterns in the data, the tool is intended to help organizations better target outreach, refine assistance, and identify perpetrators of equity stripping and deed theft scams. Furthermore, our conversations with partner organizations raised additional opportunities for increased data transparency to uncover fraudulent activity with timely data updates. More detailed data can help unlock more systematic outreach and enforcement efforts.

Technical Appendix
Scope and Methodology
This analysis covers 1-3 unit properties in New York City, excluding Staten Island, condominiums, and cooperative housing due to data limitations. 
The analysis is informed by engagement with partner organizations that work with homeowners on title and other related issues. Through focus groups, partners provided feedback on scenarios we should incorporate into our work.
Identifying High-Risk Properties and Flagged Transfers
Within this universe, we define high-risk properties as properties with a human owner that have at least one listed owner who is deceased and have either no deed recorded against the property at all or a most recent deed that is at least 30 years old (from 1996 or earlier). We identify these properties as at an elevated risk of title issues and other factors that may complicate future ownership and leave these properties vulnerable to predatory tactics. We also include a close analysis of certain types of human-to-entity transfers, which move some or all ownership from an individual to an entity or LLC. 16 Because many such transfers occur legitimately, in this analysis we focus on transfer patterns discussed with practitioners familiar with scenarios that would indicate a suspicious transaction.
Human Ownership
To identify properties with a human owner, we developed a name-classification model utilizing ownership data from a combination of the NYC Department of Finance’s Automated City Register Information System (ACRIS) and Department of City Planning’s (DCP) PLUTO. The model is a class-balanced logistic Regression that uses TF-IDF 17 alongside a set of key terms and character combinations (e.g., LLC, INC, CORP, LLP, LTD) to determine whether the listed owner is a person or an organization. Given a name, the model outputs a binary classification (PERSON or ORGANIZATION) accompanied by a confidence score. Names with a confidence level below 90 percent are flagged for manual review by our team.
Deceased Owners
To identify deceased owners, we take properties we have determined are human-owned and use the Melissa deceased suppression service to flag deceased owners within our data. Due to data constraints, we are only provided with a binary indicator of whether an owner is deceased, not when they may have passed.
Violations
To assess the extent of physical distress in our properties of interest, we rely on complaint and violation data collected by the New York City Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD) and Department of Buildings (DOB) and DOB’s Environmental Control Board (ECB). We report violations based on the date they were filed, whether they remain open or have since been closed.
Violations issued by HPD inspectors are for conditions that have been confirmed to violate the New York City Housing Maintenance Code (HMC) or New York State Multiple Dwelling Law (MDL). Violations are issued in four classes: Class A (non-hazardous), Class B (hazardous), Class C (immediately hazardous), and Class I (information orders). DOB and ECB violations are issued for conditions that do not comply with the city’s Construction Code, such as boiler, facade, and fire safety violations.
Tax Delinquencies and Tax Lien Sale Status
To determine tax delinquencies, we use DOF’s Property Charges Balance data. A given property is classified as tax delinquent if it has a combined open charge of over $500 for at least 1 year. To determine tax lien sale status, we reviewed all properties that appeared on the 90-day, 60-day, 30-day, and 10-day notices and the final sale list for the following tax lien sales: 2011-2019, 2021, and 2025 (the analysis of tax lien status for high-risk properties is limited to the 2025 tax lien sale).
Data and Analysis Limitations
Focus group participants raised some scenarios that could not be reviewed due to the structure of the available data. For example, we could not filter transactions by the type of deed used (e.g., quitclaim vs. warranty deeds), nor could we incorporate information from Surrogate’s Court proceedings about a property. We were also unable to include sales or transfers of condominium or cooperative units, for reasons we discuss thoroughly in our prior publication.

2 HOME EQUITY AT RISK
Introduction
For many New Yorkers, homeownership represents their most significant investment 
and a valuable asset to pass on to the next generation. As homeowners age and home 
values appreciate rapidly, incomplete property records and title issues may make 
some homeowners vulnerable to exploitation. When ownership or other property 
records are unclear, residents may be more susceptible to title-related complications,  
legal challenges, or pressure to sell under unfavorable terms. 
In partnership with a number of organizations and government agencies that work closely with homeowners, 
this brief leverages large data sets to track how many homeowners in New York City are at risk of title 
problems and other issues that may lead to a loss of equity. This analysis provides an updated look at 
the potential scale of the problem and the extent to which title issues overlap with other indicators of 
homeowner financial distress. It also provides insights on which neighborhoods may be most at risk.
This data brief builds on earlier work to better understand the risks facing long-time New York City 
homeowners and inform policy responses. In our 2025 brief, Using Data to Prevent the Loss of Home 
Equity in New York City , the NYU Furman Center estimated the number and geographic distribution 
of 1-3 family homes in New York City with indicators that suggested vulnerability to predatory tactics 
or title-related issues. Since then, the NYU Furman Center has met with government officials, legal 
services providers, nonprofit partners, and law enforcement officials to understand how additional 
risk indicators should be included in the analysis. Simultaneously, the NYU Furman Center created a 
restricted-access mapping tool that uses these data to help these institutions provide support to at-risk 
homeowners. This data brief both updates the data published last year and explains and estimates the 
prevalence of additional indicators that could signal homeowner risk.

Recent Policy and Research on  
Deed Theft and Title Issues
In recent years, policymakers have prioritized addressing and preventing deed theft, heirs’ property 
partition actions, and other title-related issues. In one notable example, in April of this year, Mayor 
Mamdani established the Mayor’s Office of Deed Theft Prevention, housed in the Department of 
Finance. This new office is tasked with coordinating an interagency effort to prevent, detect, investigate,  
and respond to deed theft.1 
Elected officials in New York have also enacted a number of laws targeting specific heirs’ property 
and deed theft scenarios and protecting vulnerable populations. New York’s state-level heirs’ property  
legislation, enacted in 2019,2 is based on the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (UPHPA), which 
was approved by the Uniform Law Commission in 2010.3  
In 2024, New York State enacted the Heirs Property Protection and Deed Theft Prevention Act. This 
legislation strengthened the 2019 law by expanding protections for heirs’ property owners and barring 
non-heir purchasers with a fractional interest in heirs’ property from initiating a partition action. The 
act also increased penalties for deed theft and introduced Transfer on Death Deeds (TODDs).4 A TODD 
is a low-cost estate planning tool that allows property owners to designate a beneficiary to automatically 
inherit the property upon the owner’s death, with no need to go through probate for the transferred 
property.5 Other recent state-level legislation also focuses on deed theft.6 
Alongside this continued legislative interest in protecting heirs’ property owners and preventing deed 
theft of vulnerable properties, recent research explores the prevalence of title issues and heirs’ property in 
New York City. In our 2025 brief, we drew on public and proprietary data to identify 1-3 unit residential 
properties with either at least one deceased owner or no recorded ownership transfers or mortgage activity 
since 1995. Properties that exhibited one of these indicators were flagged as “at risk,” while properties

Title-Related Vulnerabilities
This section focuses on properties that are at high risk of title-related vulnerabilities (including heirs’

When a title is unclear, it can be difficult for owners to lay claim to and fully leverage the value of their 
home. A clear title is often required to use the property as collateral for a loan, enroll in tax relief programs, 
or otherwise use the home as a financial asset, and properties with unclear title cannot receive FEMA 
disaster recovery funds or enroll in many home repair programs. 
Additionally, given New York City’s tight real estate market and price appreciation over time, many 
of these properties have significantly increased in value since they were purchased. Aging longtime 
residents or recent inheritors of a fractional interest in the property may not know the current market 
value of their home and, because of unclear title, they may be more vulnerable to scams when presented 
with lower offers from a predatory actor.

Transfer on Death Deeds
Transfer on Death Deeds are an important new tool in estate planning that became available in 2024 as  
part of the Heirs Property Protection and Deed Theft Prevention Act. During our focus groups, practitioners 
noted that it would be useful to track uptake of this option, especially among homeowners at high risk 
of having unclear titles. 
When an owner records a TODD, heirs will directly inherit a property without having to go through 
probate upon the owner’s death. This form of estate planning is a low-cost way to ensure that a property 
passes directly and securely to designated beneficiaries, avoiding potentially complicated and expensive 
court proceedings and helping to prevent tangled title issues.

Uptake of Transfer on Death Deeds has risen since their introduction 
in 2024, though the overall volume is low. 
Figure 1 shows the number of TODDs recorded across New York City and its boroughs, not including Staten 
Island, on a monthly basis, from the introduction of TODDs in July 2024 to July 2026. In the second half 
of 2024, 181 TODDs were recorded citywide; over 2025, a total of 713 TODDs were filed. Between January 
and July 2026, 486 TODDs had been filed. It is not immediately clear why recorded TODDs spiked in 
some spring or summer months in 2025 and 2026. Note that in our prior work, we found approximately 
45,000 properties for which there was no recorded deed or mortgage activity since 1995 or at least one 
deceased owner was still listed as an owner of record. Based on those indicators, we might assume the 
homeowners for those 45,000 properties are older or that the home was, for example, previously passed 
to an aging spouse. Though still underinclusive of the overall need, this data point still suggests there 
may be a substantial number of homes in need of further outreach about the TODD option. 
At a borough level, TODD uptake follows the distribution of 1-3 unit properties, with Queens and Brooklyn 
recording far more TODDs than Manhattan. TODD numbers may rise as legal services organizations 
and government officials continue to introduce this new estate planning option to homeowners.

Data Transparency and Other 
Considerations
Our analysis tracks indicators of vulnerability for property owners, including risk of title issues and deed 
theft. Based on focus group conversations with stakeholders, we were able to identify a number of such 
scenarios in the data. However, those conversations also surfaced a number of scenarios and indicators 
that we cannot identify given currently available data. To gain more insight into these scenarios, we 
recommend updating the publicly readable data to specify the deed type and whether properties go 
through Surrogate’s Court. In addition, it would be helpful to collect machine-readable information on 
the notaries or attorneys who presented the recorded document so we could identify patterns and help 
determine whether specific networks or actors are repetitively engaging in suspicious conduct. In order 
to avoid compromising the effectiveness of these recommendations, we do not outline them in detail here. 
We will share our recommendations for improving data transparency directly with the relevant agencies. 
We created our interactive map and dashboard detailing at-risk households to assist government officials, 
legal service providers and law enforcement in identifying the scale of the issue at a neighborhood level. 
The protected map includes property-level information to help partners target their outreach. As we 
outlined in our 2025 brief, Using Data to Prevent the Loss of Home Equity in New York City, that outreach 
should be performed by trusted organizations that have deep local relationships with the communities 
that we have identified as at risk. Local organizations can help ensure that outreach is also carried out 
in a multilingual and culturally competent manner. Outreach can inform homeowners of the need for 
estate planning (including tools like TODDs), warn them of scams, and connect them with trusted legal 
providers. Furthermore, outreach can direct homeowners to programs that provide financial support 
for property taxes, water charges, home repairs, and disaster relief.
Directing homeowners to current resources and programs, while effective in many ways, is not enough 
to protect vulnerable homeowners. For a more comprehensive discussion of policy recommendations 
and data considerations that can help protect at-risk homeowners, see our 2025 brief.

Conclusion
Our analysis leverages updated data to better understand the scale and geography of properties that 
are vulnerable to or may have experienced equity stripping, a topic we first explored in our 2025 brief.  
We identify thousands of properties at risk of tangled title issues. Those properties also show heightened 
signs of financial distress, raising concerns about underlying challenges these homeowners are facing. 
High-risk properties are seven times more likely to show up on the City’s most recent tax lien sale list and 
six times more likely to be delinquent on property taxes than all 1-3 unit properties owned by individuals. 
In an encouraging sign, we find that 1,380 Transfer on Death Deeds that can help lower homeowner risk  
have been filed between July 2024, when the tool was implemented, and July 2026. 
Across four focus groups with legal services providers, city government leaders, nonprofit partners, and 
law enforcement agencies, we consistently heard about types of human-to-entity property transfers 
that raise concerns about potential scams or other schemes targeting homeowners. Our analysis 
shows that these transfers have risen over the last few years. They are heavily concentrated in a few 
neighborhoods, including Jamaica/Hollis and Queens Village in Queens. Similar to high-risk  properties, 
we find that properties with human-to-entity transfers where there is a gap in the chain of title are also 
much more likely to have an indicator of financial distress. Finally, neighborhoods with the highest 
concentrations of flagged transfers also have higher shares of Black, Asian, and foreign-born residents than  
other parts of the city.
Our data show that the risk of unclear title issues remains significant, and the data also help identify 
the most vulnerable neighborhoods so our partners can better target resources and outreach. The same 
data underlying this analysis are currently available in a restricted, detailed dashboard and mapping 
tool for our partner organizations engaging in critical legal assistance and enforcement activities. 
By surfacing patterns in the data, the tool is intended to help organizations better target outreach, 
refine assistance, and identify perpetrators of equity stripping and deed theft scams. Furthermore, our 
conversations with partner organizations raised additional opportunities for increased data transparency 
to uncover fraudulent activity with timely data updates. More detailed data can help unlock more  
systematic outreach and enforcement efforts.

Technical Appendix
Scope and Methodology
This analysis covers 1-3 unit properties in New York City, excluding Staten Island, condominiums, and 
cooperative housing due to data limitations. 
The analysis is informed by engagement with partner organizations that work with homeowners on 
title and other related issues. Through focus groups, partners provided feedback on scenarios we should 
incorporate into our work.
Identifying High-Risk Properties and Flagged Transfers
Within this universe, we define high-risk properties as properties with a human owner that have at 
least one listed owner who is deceased and have either no deed recorded against the property at all or a 
most recent deed that is at least 30 years old (from 1996 or earlier). We identify these properties as at 
an elevated risk of title issues and other factors that may complicate future ownership and leave these 
properties vulnerable to predatory tactics. We also include a close analysis of certain types of human-
to-entity transfers, which move some or all ownership from an individual to an entity or LLC.16 Because 
many such transfers occur legitimately, in this analysis we focus on transfer patterns discussed with 
practitioners familiar with scenarios that would indicate a suspicious transaction. 
Human Ownership
To identify properties with a human owner, we developed a name-classification model utilizing ownership 
data from a combination of the NYC Department of Finance’s Automated City Register Information 
System (ACRIS) and Department of City Planning’s (DCP) PLUTO. The model is a class-balanced logistic 
Regression that uses TF-IDF17 alongside a set of key terms and character combinations (e.g., LLC, INC, 
CORP, LLP, LTD) to determine whether the listed owner is a person or an organization. Given a name, 
the model outputs a binary classification (PERSON or ORGANIZATION) accompanied by a confidence 
score. Names with a confidence level below 90 percent are flagged for manual review by our team. 
16. Based on practitioner feedback, we limit our analysis to “high-risk” properties, those with both at least one deceased homeowner and 
no recorded documents in the last 30 years. We do not cover the “at-risk” category which we explored in our original analysis, homes 
which met only one of the risk criteria, due to the concern that it is overly broad. 
17. Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) is a natural language processing (NLP) method used in topic modeling and 
text analysis that quantifies a word’s importance in a given document. To calculate importance, the method takes the frequency of a 
word in a document—in this case, names of listed owners of 1-3 residential unit properties in New York City—and divides that value by 
the number of documents in a given corpus that contain that word. Through this process, names using “LLC,” “Inc.,” or “Corporation,” 
will be weighted less than a unique first and last name. Common surnames were sent through additional screenings to adjust the 
weights of their names. As the first step of the modeling, TF-IDF allows for the model to filter out the most common entity indicators. 
For more information, see Spärck Jones, K. (1972). A statistical interpretation of term specificity and its application in retrieval. Journal 
of Documentation, 28(1), 11–21. https://www.staff.city.ac.uk/~sbrp622/idfpapers/ksj_orig.pdf.

Deceased Owners
To identify deceased owners, we take properties we have determined are human-owned and use the Melissa 
deceased suppression service to flag deceased owners within our data. Due to data constraints, we are 
only provided with a binary indicator of whether an owner is deceased, not when they may have passed. 
Violations
To assess the extent of physical distress in our properties of interest, we rely on complaint and violation 
data collected by the New York City Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD) and 
Department of Buildings (DOB) and DOB’s Environmental Control Board (ECB). We report violations 
based on the date they were filed, whether they remain open or have since been closed.
Violations issued by HPD inspectors are for conditions that have been confirmed to violate the New York 
City Housing Maintenance Code (HMC) or New York State Multiple Dwelling Law (MDL). Violations are 
issued in four classes: Class A (non-hazardous), Class B (hazardous), Class C (immediately hazardous), 
and Class I (information orders). DOB and ECB violations are issued for conditions that do not comply 
with the city’s Construction Code, such as boiler, facade, and fire safety violations. 
Tax Delinquencies and Tax Lien Sale Status
To determine tax delinquencies, we use DOF’s Property Charges Balance data. A given property is classified 
as tax delinquent if it has a combined open charge of over $500 for at least 1 year. To determine tax lien 
sale status, we reviewed all properties that appeared on the 90-day, 60-day, 30-day, and 10-day notices 
and the final sale list for the following tax lien sales: 2011-2019, 2021, and 2025 (the analysis of tax lien 
status for high-risk properties is limited to the 2025 tax lien sale). 
Data and Analysis Limitations
Focus group participants raised some scenarios that could not be reviewed due to the structure of the 
available data. For example, we could not filter transactions by the type of deed used (e.g., quitclaim 
vs. warranty deeds), nor could we incorporate information from Surrogate’s Court proceedings about 
a property. We were also unable to include sales or transfers of condominium or cooperative units  
for reasons we discuss thoroughly in our prior publication.
